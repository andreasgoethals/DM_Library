# Decision models: source summaries

The selected Zotero **Decision Models** collection contains sixteen research papers
and fifteen web-only releases/model cards. The current evidence concerns fast,
schema-conditioned probabilistic decisions. It does not constitute a general
survey of decision theory or optimization. Read [the synthesis](SYNTHESIS.md) for
comparisons, [the model comparison](DECISION_MODELS.md) for specifications and
costs, and [the implementation guide](REPOSITORIES.md) for source maps.

Dates below describe the PDF version filed. Publication dates remain separately
recorded in [the provenance manifest](papers/manifest.json); multiple October
versions are ordered by their confirmed day. All sixteen are arXiv preprints: no
peer-reviewed venue is confirmed in the curated records. Source-specific numbers
are reported results, not measurements rerun by this library. Citation keys come
from Zotero and remain unchanged.

Every paper entry uses the same order: provenance, citation key, **Where it fits**
(position in the corpus), **What it contains** (method and protocol), **Key
results** (the paper's own headline findings with table or section pointers),
**Strengths**, **Limitations** (author-stated points separated from this
library's reading where useful) and **Evidence locations**. Web-source entries
use the same labels, and their contents are marked as provider claims.

## 2026-09 — Li et al. — Beyond Calibration: Do a Typed-Decision Model's Probabilities Obey the Probability Axioms?

**Venue / provenance:** arXiv preprint `2609.33209v1`; filed PDF version dated 2026-09-27 (arXiv banner; the typeset title page says 29 September). Zotero publication date: 2026-09-27. No peer-reviewed venue recorded. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2609.33209](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2609.33209). [PDF](papers/2026/09_Li_et_al._Beyond_Calibration_Do_a_Typed_Decision_Models_Probabilities_Obey_the_Probability_Axioms.pdf) · [generated text](papers/text/2026/09_Li_et_al._Beyond_Calibration_Do_a_Typed_Decision_Models_Probabilities_Obey_the_Probability_Axioms.txt).

**Zotero citation key:** `2026.LI.calibrationtypeddecisionmodelsprobabilitiesobeyprobabilityaxioms`

**Where it fits.** Li, He and Li (2026), *Beyond Calibration: Do a Typed-Decision Model's Probabilities Obey the Probability Axioms?*, separate calibration (agreement with outcome frequencies across many cases) from coherence (agreement of the probabilities a model gives to logically related questions about the same case). A pipeline that thresholds or combines several probabilities about one case needs the second property, which accuracy and calibration metrics do not test.

**What it contains.** Each of 160 items (100 ChaosNLI items with entailment/neutral/contradiction; 60 expert-labelled PubMedQA items, answers renamed affirmative/negative/inconclusive to avoid questions such as "is the label not no?") has a state and an explicit partition into three mutually exclusive, exhaustive labels, restated in a fixed definition paragraph. For each label X the battery asks three yes/no questions — "the label is X" (s), "the label is not X" (n), "the label is X_i or X_j" (d) — plus one choice question (c). Any single probability measure implies s + n = 1, s + d = 1, the three s sum to 1, d = n, monotonicity and s = c (Eq. 1), so every check is label-free. The primary measure is the item-averaged absolute negation violation |s + n − 1|. Jev 1.13 is queried through OpenRouter, one question per request and every request repeated once to estimate a repeat-noise floor (Eq. 3); a bundling check sends all ten questions in one request. The comparison is Qwen3.8-27B (official BF16 weights, thinking disabled) under two readouts: first-token probabilities renormalized over the answer tokens, and probabilities stated in JSON. Four confound checks address confidence (pairs where both systems give similar s), readout, double-negation labels (contradiction, negative) and repeat noise. The 3,360 Jev calls cost US$0.076.

**Key results.** Over 480 negation pairs the mean violation is 0.064 [0.055, 0.072] for Jev, 0.293 for Qwen's first-token readout and 0.122 for its verbalized readout (Table 1). The gap to the first-token readout is unchanged on confidence-matched pairs (Jev 0.028 vs 0.257) and grows without double-negation labels (Table 2). Jev is not coherent: its violation is about five times its noise floor of 0.013, and it over-endorses single-label statements (mean 0.380 where 1/3 is required), so its three single-label probabilities sum to 1.14 and exceed one on 159 of 160 items; its yes/no and choice probabilities for the same label differ by 0.091 (Tables 3–4). The failure modes differ: the first-token readout says "no" to both a statement and its negation in 196 of 480 pairs and does not become more coherent when confident, whereas Jev's violation falls from 0.142 on uncertain pairs to 0.018 on its most confident ones (Figure 3). Bundling all questions into one request changes Jev's answers no more than repeating a request.

**Strengths.** Explicit partitions make each identity follow from the question text alone. The measured noise floor and the confidence, readout and double-negation controls rule out the obvious artefactual explanations of the gap. The decomposition by question form (Table 4) localizes systematic bias without labels, which a choice-format calibration report cannot see because choice outputs are normalized. Stimuli, raw logs and analysis scripts are released.

**Limitations.** Author-stated: two English domains, one wording each for negation and disjunction and no paraphrase control (the two complement wordings differ by 0.060, about as much as the negation violation); one Jev version via one gateway; one comparison model with thinking disabled; public datasets, so memorization cannot be excluded; coherence is necessary but not sufficient, since constant answers of 1/3 and 2/3 pass every check. Reviewer reading: Table 4's decomposition and several correlations are labelled exploratory, and per-label estimates rest on 19–100 items.

**Evidence locations.** §§3–7; Eqs. 1–3; Tables 1–4; Figures 1–3. The [author supplement](https://github.com/bro789/typed-decision-coherence) is linked for reproduction but is not selected as a substantial maintained implementation snapshot.

## 2026-09 — Deußer et al. — Evaluating and Benchmarking the System One Model Jev

**Venue / provenance:** arXiv preprint `2609.37647v1`; filed PDF version dated 2026-09-29. Zotero publication date: 2026-09-29. No peer-reviewed venue recorded. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2609.37647](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2609.37647). [PDF](papers/2026/09_Deusser_et_al._Evaluating_and_Benchmarking_the_System_One_Model_Jev.pdf) · [generated text](papers/text/2026/09_Deusser_et_al._Evaluating_and_Benchmarking_the_System_One_Model_Jev.txt).

**Zotero citation key:** `2026.DEUSSER.evaluatingbenchmarkingsystemonemodeljev`

**Where it fits.** Deußer, Sparrenberg and Sifa (2026), *Evaluating and Benchmarking the System One Model Jev*, provide the first broad public-benchmark baseline for a hosted typed-decision service. The contribution is measurement across Jev's three question primitives and many task families, with reusable raw responses, rather than a new model.

**What it contains.** Thirty-seven public datasets in seven categories (classification, routing, NLI/grounding, reading comprehension and knowledge, commonsense, safety/moderation/legal, rubric scoring) are mapped onto Jev's primitives: Choice (pick one of up to 255 options), Noul (probability of "yes"; multi-label tasks become one Noul per label in one request) and Score (probability over a 2–10-level described rubric, summarized as a probability-weighted level) (§§3.1–3.3; Table 1). Jev 1.13.0 is queried zero-shot on full evaluation splits (346,009 requests) with one template per dataset, piloted on 20 training/validation examples and then frozen. Two open-weight controls, Qwen3.8-27B and Gemma-4-E4B, receive identical requests and are scored by their exact next-token probabilities restricted to single-token option codes and renormalized, with thinking disabled (§3.6) — a matched probability readout rather than generated JSON. Metrics include task-appropriate primary scores with bootstrap intervals, 15-bin ECE, Brier score, selective-prediction accuracy and AURC, thresholds tuned on 1,000 training examples, and two memorization probes (option rotation, question withheld).

**Key results.** Jev beats Qwen on 27 datasets, trails on nine and ties one; eleven Jev leads have non-overlapping 95% intervals, none of Qwen's do, and Jev exceeds Gemma on all 37 (Table 3). It reaches 95–99% on IMDB, SST-2, HellaSwag and ARC, and 86.7% on Belebele across 122 languages, with a steep drop on low-resource languages (§4.5). Pooled Choice ECE is 0.028 over 279,925 answers (median per-dataset 0.028, unweighted mean 0.061), with miscalibration concentrated on low-accuracy datasets (Figure 2; Appendix Table 8). Binary interfaces err in opposite directions: single Nouls are under-confident (mean P(yes) 0.465 vs a 0.518 base rate), explaining recall deficits despite high AUROC, while multi-label fan-out over-predicts yes (0.209 vs 0.041). Thresholds tuned on training data raise UNFAIR-ToS micro-F1 from 0.499 to 0.748 (Table 4). At 50% coverage, confidence ranking lifts Banking77 accuracy from 79.7% to 96.3% (Figure 3). Rubric scoring is the one area where Qwen is on par or slightly ahead (higher correlation on 9 of 12 dimensions; Table 5). Jev scores calculation-heavy MMLU subjects higher than other MMLU subjects (94.3% vs 91.3%), unlike both open models and unlike C-Eval; option rotation leaves this unchanged and withholding the question drops it to near chance (Table 6). The main run cost US$9.15 (US$9.40 including pilot and extra analyses; Table 7).

**Strengths.** Full evaluation splits, explicit primitive mappings, frozen prompts, bootstrap intervals and matched probability readouts make the comparison auditable. The study reports calibration, selective prediction, threshold transfer, multilingual breakdowns and contamination probes rather than average accuracy alone. A content-addressed harness and all raw responses (Zenodo DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23039006) allow reanalysis without repeat API calls.

**Limitations.** Author-stated (§5): one untuned template per dataset; open models scored without reasoning on prompts written for Jev; no generative proprietary baselines; thresholds tuned on only 1,000 examples; the probes cannot detect memorized question–answer pairs, so the MMLU mathematics result remains unexplained; a single run per request; client-side latency including network time; results tied to jev-1.13.0. Reviewer reading: pooled ECE is dominated by three large datasets, and Jev's architecture and training data are unknown, so the open-model comparison places Jev on a scale without attributing differences to any property of the model.

**Evidence locations.** §§3.1–3.7; Tables 1–7; Figures 2–6; §§4.3–4.7; §5; Appendix Table 8. See [official code source maps](REPOSITORIES.md).

## 2026-09 — Zhang et al. — AnyJev Technical Report

**Venue / provenance:** arXiv preprint `2610.00831v1`; filed PDF version dated 2026-09-30. Zotero publication date: 2026-09-30. No peer-reviewed venue recorded; the report describes itself as an early report on work in development. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2610.00831](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2610.00831). [PDF](papers/2026/09_Zhang_et_al._AnyJev_Technical_Report.pdf) · [generated text](papers/text/2026/09_Zhang_et_al._AnyJev_Technical_Report.txt).

**Zotero citation key:** `2026.ZHANG.anyjevtechnicalreport`

**Where it fits.** Zhang et al. (2026), *AnyJev Technical Report*, ask how much typed-decision capability can be read from unchanged open instruction-tuned LLMs by fixing the readout alone. The contribution is an inference procedure and its serving cost, not a trained decision model; it is the training-free end of the spectrum that [Li and Wagle](#llm-as-jev-llms-are-already-jev-style-decision-models-when-and-how-to-fine-tune-them) and [Geuter et al.](#releasing-the-inner-jev-a-simple-recipe-for-open-decision-models) extend with fine-tuning.

**What it contains.** The prompt lists the options under single-token position labels (letters, Yes/No or digits); one prefill gives the next-token distribution, which is restricted to the label tokens and renormalized (Eq. 1). The normalizer, the "answer mass", is reported and used as a gate to catch template faults. Two readout defects are corrected without gradient steps (§4). A label prior, estimated by batch calibration (the running mean of the model's own restricted outputs on already-scored unlabelled inputs, per question and layout), is divided out with strength α = 0.75 (Eq. 2); a content-free-probe estimator exists but is not used. Position bias is removed by reading all K cyclic rotations of the option list and averaging log-probabilities, which is exact if the logit separates into an option term plus a position term (Eq. 3). Confidence calibration is a separate single temperature fitted on 100–500 labelled examples. Labels therefore enter in two places: α (chosen once from a sweep over 230 model–question pairs scored against gold labels) and the temperature. To cut the K-prefill cost, an adaptive budget reads rotations in a well-spread (van der Corput) order and stops when the top-two log-probability margin reaches a threshold; the threshold is chosen on unlabelled states so that the stopped decision agrees with the full-rotation decision with a Clopper–Pearson bound, i.e. the guarantee is agreement with the model's own full readout, not accuracy (§5). Experiments use 13 open models (1.7B–32B) in bf16 on one H100.

**Key results.** On two 20-option tasks (banking20, 20 Newsgroups; 11 models, 300 items per cell), rotations lower the reversal flip rate from 0.328 to 0.138 and from 0.334 to 0.183 and raise accuracy on 11 of 11 models (mean +7.2 and +5.9 points); calibration error falls mainly with the temperature (0.251 to 0.088 on banking20), not the rotations (Table 1; Figure 2). On the JevBench public subset (213 of 231 items, 2–6 options; label prior off, so this isolates rotations) accuracy spans 0.549–0.798 across eight models and rotations have no measurable effect: +0.006 [−0.004, +0.018] pooled over 1,704 decisions; temperature improves ECE on seven of eight models (Table 2; Figure 3). On the full BEV Decision Mix test split (46,320 decisions; Appendix E), rotations add little (68.01% vs 67.85% raw on Qwen3.5-9B), the label prior lowers accuracy to 65.01%, and the fine-tuned Bespoke-Nimble-9B reaches 70.57% (§7.3). Serving an 18-option question on vLLM, the adaptive budget reads 7.3 of 18 rotations, serves 2.22× as many decisions per second and returns the full-rotation decision 98.7% of the time (Table 3). Negative results: a question-agnostic linear head, cache reuse of the question block and an option mask that makes options exchangeable (accuracy falls to chance) all fail (§8).

**Strengths.** Unchanged weights isolate readout effects, and the report states exactly where labels enter. The answer-mass gate exposes silent template failures. It separates debiasing from calibration and one prefill per rotation from one prefill per decision, and documents negative results. Code is public.

**Limitations.** Author-stated (§9): one prefill cannot reason (hardest JevBench tier 0.343–0.590); the public benchmark has few options, where rotations matter least; 18 items are unscored; the 20-option results lack item-level intervals; intervals cover items, not runs; and accuracy and efficiency are measured on different tasks. The 7.3-rotation serving run selects and bounds its threshold on the same states, so its bound does not cover the selected threshold; with disjoint selection and verification the same cell needs 10.6 rotations, and only two of four cells certify (§5; Table 10). Reviewer reading: the label-prior correction helps on the in-house 20-option tasks but hurts on the external full split, so its default use is task-dependent; no accuracy or regret guarantee is offered.

**Evidence locations.** §§3–9; Eqs. 1–5; Tables 1–3 and 10–11; Figures 2–3; Appendices A, D and E (Table 14). See [AnyJev source map](REPOSITORIES.md#anyjevtxt).

## 2026-10 — Cao et al. — SearchJev: A Fast and Calibrated System-1 Model for Search Agents

**Venue / provenance:** arXiv preprint `2610.05107v1`; filed PDF version dated 2026-10-04. Zotero publication date: 2026-10-04. No peer-reviewed venue recorded. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2610.05107](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2610.05107). [PDF](papers/2026/10_Cao_et_al._SearchJev_A_Fast_and_Calibrated_System_1_Model_for_Search_Agents.pdf) · [generated text](papers/text/2026/10_Cao_et_al._SearchJev_A_Fast_and_Calibrated_System_1_Model_for_Search_Agents.txt).

**Zotero citation key:** `2026.CAO.searchjevfastcalibratedsystem1modelsearchagents`

**Where it fits.** Cao et al. (2026), *SearchJev: A Fast and Calibrated System-1 Model for Search Agents*, train a domain-specific decision model for the short judgments inside a search agent and embed it in a hybrid controller: a larger model keeps planning, query writing and answer composition, while the decision model handles bounded judgments and is meant to delegate uncertain ones. It is a component inside a hand-designed controller, not an autonomous policy learner.

**What it contains.** SearchDecision-Bench converts Chinese and English resources into state–schema pairs for six decision types (routing, rewriting, relevance, sufficiency, navigation, verification) with Choice, Score (ordinal) and Noul (binary) outputs; four types have held-out out-of-distribution (OOD) test sets (Tables 1–2). Splits are isolated by identity keys and query text, and synthetic variants of evaluation sources are discarded (§2.2). The model is a Qwen3.5 backbone (0.8B or 4B) that labels each option with a single token and reads the first-output-position logits of those labels through the existing LM head, normalized over legal options only (Eq. 1), so no JSON is generated. Soft-Label Learning for Calibrated Decisions (SLCD) trains these distributions with LoRA against source-dependent soft targets, combining cross-entropy and Brier loss (λ = 0.5) and down-weighting LLM-assigned or constructed labels (Eq. 2); a separate temperature per output type is then fitted on 5,000 held-out validation decisions (Eq. 3). In the agent, a confidence gate accepts a decision when its top calibrated probability reaches a threshold δ and otherwise asks the larger model; a rule-based controller maps decisions to search/open/rerank/stop (§3.3). Baselines are the same untrained backbones with constrained JSON decoding and Jev 1.13 via API.

**Key results.** In-distribution, both sizes beat same-size JSON baselines on all six families, run 5.2–5.3× faster (median 27.6/37.1 ms vs 144.2/195.2 ms at batch size 1) and cut mean ECE by 74.4% (0.8B) and 40.7% (4B) (Table 3). Against Jev 1.13 they win on sufficiency, routing, rewriting and verification, tie on relevance, and lose on navigation (Jev 72.1 vs 57.2 for 4B); Jev keeps the lowest mean ECE. On 100 sampled BrowseComp-Plus questions, run once each with Qwen3.5-27B as the larger model and BM25 retrieval, answer accuracy is 45% for the large model alone (median active time 4,080 s), 46% with Jev as the decision model (1,018 s), 46% with the 0.8B model (868 s, fewest large-model calls) and 54% with the 4B model (1,102 s) (Table 4). OOD, routing improves by about 12 points over the JSON baselines, but calibration transfer is uneven and 4B verification falls below its JSON baseline (65.0 vs 78.0; Table 5).

**Strengths.** A proper-scoring objective is tied to an explicit accept-or-escalate rule, and the evaluation reports final answer quality alongside local accuracy, large-model calls, output tokens and time. Holding the larger reasoner fixed and including a Jev-based hybrid makes the system comparison interpretable. Task-level and held-out results are reported separately rather than treating calibration as universal.

**Limitations.** Author-stated (Appendix H): Chinese and English text only; options must be enumerable; LLMs help label about a third of training rows. Reviewer reading: one run on 100 questions supports only a limited deployment claim. Table 8 (Appendix E.2) records zero explicit large-model short-decision calls in both SearchJev runs, and the logs cannot recover δ (Appendix D.3), so the gain cannot be attributed to uncertainty delegation. Task-specific training and the switch from JSON decoding to direct readout change together, so baseline gains do not isolate either. No OOD sufficiency or navigation tests exist. In the leaderboard comparison of Appendix F (self-reported peer numbers), the 4B model scores below its own untrained backbone on the hard JevBench tier (62.2 vs 65.8), and far below peers on RAGTruth.

**Evidence locations.** Abstract; §§2.2, 3.1–3.3, 4.4 and 5.1–5.3; Eqs. 1–3; Tables 2–5; Appendices D.3, E.2 (Table 8), F (Table 9) and H. See [official code source maps](REPOSITORIES.md).

## 2026-10 — Rafe and Das — Benchmarking System One decision models against trained classifiers and language models for automated decision gates

**Venue / provenance:** arXiv preprint `2610.00346v2`; filed PDF version dated 2026-10-06. Zotero publication date: 2026-09-29 (v1). **Where it fits.** Rafe and Das (2026), *Benchmarking System One decision models against trained classifiers and language models for automated decision gates*, evaluate decision models as components of a gate: a program that accepts a declared option, rejects an out-of-scope request or escalates a doubtful one. **What it contains.** One harness sends the same typed request (shared state, questions, option texts in fixed order; neutral option identifiers o1…oK unless stated) to eight checkpoints from six decision-model families (Jev 1.13.0, two Laya encoders, Kev-0.8B/9B, decider-2b, this-that-model-1.0, Nimble-9B) and to four open generative comparators (Qwen3-14B, Qwen3.6-27B, Gemma-4-31B, Mistral-Small-24B). Unreadable replies are scored as uniform distributions. Jev is near-calibrated on typed-decisions and CLINC-150 (ECE 0.033, 0.032) but not on the social-science tasks (pooled 0.251, 0.038 after scaling); Kev-9B's stored temperature raises its CLINC-150 ECE from 0.051 to 0.377 (Table 7). **Where it fits.** Xu et al. (2026), *JevOut: Natural Context Can Flip Decision Models*, test the input side of decision reliability: whether short, natural-looking additions that leave the correct answer unchanged can redirect a constrained decision to a chosen wrong option. It complements calibration and interface studies such as [Li et al. on probability coherence](#beyond-calibration-do-a-typed-decision-model-s-probabilities-obey-the-probability-axioms) and the wording-perturbation suites of [TypedBench](#typedbench-a-benchmark-for-calibration-framing-sensitivity-and-cost-in-system-one-decision-models).

**What it contains.** For each target model, only items it initially answers correctly are kept, and the most probable wrong option is fixed as the target. A context addition inserts sentences at chosen boundaries while preserving every original character, the question, the options and their order. A proposer LLM (Gemma4-12B), given the item, gold answer and target, writes one sentence at a time; a separate checker invocation, blind to the target and score, accepts only additions that fit naturally, keep the same correct answer and contain no explicit answer-selection cue. Accepted contexts are scored by the target model and archived; parents for refinement are sampled with probability proportional to exp(margin/T), where the margin is the log-probability of the target over its strongest competitor, mixed with uniform exploration (Eqs. 2–4). The budget is 16 proposal slots × 4 iterations, i.e. at most 64 accepted target evaluations per decision; rejected proposals consume no target call. Success is the targeted flip rate (TFR, Eq. 1): the share of initially correct decisions for which some accepted context makes the model select the fixed target. Targets are hosted Jev 1.13.0, OpenSourceJev and a plain Qwen scorer (same frozen Qwen3-1.7B weights, different interfaces) and Von 1.0 (395M, non-autoregressive). Seven datasets (MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, MuSR, ToMBench, LAR-ECHR, SAT A-Bench as binary membership decisions, BFCL V4 tool routing at recorded checkpoints) each contribute 50 development and 100 held-out items (§§3–4.1). Proposers are also LoRA-tuned on optimization trajectories (V1 next-addition, V2 complete-context; §3.3).

**Key results.** TFR is 61.4% for Jev (n = 508 initially correct), 72.6% for OpenSourceJev (328), 73.2% for Von (328) and 64.9% for plain Qwen (285); in 45.1–64.3% of decisions the target also reaches probability ≥ 0.7 (Table 1). Initially confident Jev decisions (gold probability ≥ 0.9, n = 290) still flip 45.5% of the time (Table 2). One added sentence suffices for 41.5% of Jev decisions, and ≤ 32 added words for 40.9% (Table 3). The effect does not depend on the optimizer: repeated independent target-aware generation already reaches 48.0% on the same 508 Jev decisions, and full optimization adds 13.4 points; probability-guided parent allocation beats label-only allocation by 7.1 points on 140 matched decisions (§4.4). Frozen contexts transfer to other systems at 21.1–47.8% (Figure 5). Blinded majority judgment by three annotators finds 91.6% of 250 sampled successes valid, versus 93.0% of 100 accepted-but-unsuccessful controls (Table 7). Trained proposers raise one-generation TFR on held-out Jev items from 16.1% to 18.7% (V1) and 21.9% (V2), and best-of-four from 29.1% to 32.9% and 34.3% (Table 4). In a repeat audit, 97 of 100 sampled Jev flips recur in at least eight of ten identical calls (Appendix I.2).

**Strengths.** The fixed wrong target, retained full denominator (failures and rejections count against success), matched-budget controls, blinded human validity audit with unsuccessful controls, repeatability audit and cross-model transfer make the robustness claim precise. Measuring target probability, not only label changes, shows that redirected decisions are often confidently wrong. The shared-weights OpenSourceJev/plain-Qwen pair separates interface from backbone. Code, frozen configuration and full execution accounting are released.

**Limitations.** Author-stated: tasks are English with finite option sets and known answers; tool routing is evaluated at recorded checkpoints, so propagation through closed-loop interaction is untested. Reviewer reading: the search has access to the gold answer and the chosen target, and rejected proposals are outside the 64-call cap, so total generation cost is higher than the cap suggests (primary optimization averages 47.6 proposals and 41.9 accepted evaluations per case; Appendix I.1). TFR is a conditional search success rate on different initially-correct populations, not a natural-input error rate or a cross-model robustness leaderboard. Validity is judged on a sample of successes, and the automatic checker is the same model family as the proposer.

**Evidence locations.** §§3–4 (§4.6 for learned proposers), Limitations; Eqs. 1–4; Tables 1–4, 7 and 28–30; Figures 1–5; Appendices B–D (definitions, interfaces, acceptance checks, protocol), E–G (task-level, confidence strata, transfer), H (proposer training), I–J (accounting, repeatability, independent-generation control). **Where it fits.** Li, Miao, Krishnan and Padman (2026), *JEV-as-a-Judge: Accept When Confident, Escalate When Unsure*, study a concrete accept-or-escalate decision: Jev acts as a cheap first-stage evaluation judge, and its confidence decides whether an expensive reasoning judge is called. The contribution is an empirical operating envelope and a threshold-validation procedure, not a new routing algorithm.

**What it contains.** Jev 1.13.0 receives one Choice question per request (rubric, allowed labels, the item) and returns a probability per label; the paper's confidence q is the largest of these, not Jev's unpublished native confidence field (Spearman 0.958–0.999 with it; §4). Sixteen other configurations (fourteen generative LLM judges plus two reward models, PairRM and Skywork-Reward-V2) are compared on 5,172 base judgments: 1,500 RewardBench and 350 JudgeBench preference pairs, 3,000 HaluEval evidence-grounded factuality answers, 150 final-answer adjudications and two easy controls (§3). Generative judges answer under the same contract (verdict plus verbalized label probabilities, no rationale). The base set is split by source question into a 642-item pilot (40% selection set for temperatures and thresholds, 60% pilot test) and a 4,530-item held-out set whose 1,610 preference pairs test routing. Follow-ups cover reversed candidate order, RM-Bench style variation, four-way RewardBench 2 selection, answer format and 600 natural-prose judgments. Fees and latency come from a separate 120-judgment timing panel (§4). One team member, blind to labels and judge outputs, adjudicated all 163 items in a 990-item subset that Jev or GPT-6 Astra gets wrong under the supplied labels, plus 20 controls (183 adjudications; §6, Appendix D).

The cascade (§8) judges each pair in both candidate orders, averages the aligned probability (Eq. 1), accepts Jev's verdict when the averaged confidence reaches τ and otherwise takes the fallback judge's verdict. τ is the most permissive grid value whose accuracy loss against the fallback on labelled local items stays within two points: the point rule uses the mean paired loss; the lower-bound rule uses its one-sided 95% lower confidence bound. Both minimize cost under an accuracy tolerance rather than a priced error cost.

**Key results.** Standalone, Jev ranks eighth of fifteen LLM judges on 4,850 pooled public judgments, 1.7 points behind GPT-6, at $0.044 per 1,000 judgments (277× cheaper, i.e. 0.36% of the fee) and 0.15 s median latency (Table 1; §5). It ties GPT-6 on RewardBench (92.5%) and is within about three points on factuality and final answers, but trails by 7–28 points where the verdict must be derived (expert knowledge, code, math, logic) and by 13.5 on style-adversarial RM-Bench pairs (§6). Where Jev's confidence is at least 0.9 the two judges are nearly tied (94.7% versus 94.4% on 3,744 items; §7). The frozen two-order Jev→GPT-6 policy (τ = 0.9, chosen by the point rule on 96 pilot selection pairs before held-out inspection) escalates 31.5% of the 1,610 held-out pairs and scores 93.4% versus GPT-6's 92.5% (+0.93 points, [0.24, 1.66]) at 41.4% of its fee (Table 4). The pool is 83% RewardBench: on JudgeBench alone the policy escalates 65% and stays 0.74 points below GPT-6 at 66.8% of its fee. A live rerun on 510 held-out pairs reproduces the offline estimates with 0.27 s versus 2.10 s median latency (§8; Appendix O). In the prospective live test (§9), thresholds chosen per workload on 100 local labels by the lower-bound rule match GPT-6 exactly (90.2%) on 570 held-out PPE and JudgeBench-Claude pairs, escalating 74.2% at 75.5% of its fee (Table 5). In resampling, the lower-bound rule cuts the chance of losing more than two points from about 45% (point rule) to about 5% with roughly 100 labels.

**Strengths.** Threshold exploration, frozen selection, live replication and a prospective workload test are kept distinct, and only the latter three are pre-specified. Source-cluster bootstrap intervals, a matched-spend random-routing control, retrospective alternative first stages (Table 3), human adjudication and explicit failure cases accompany the headline numbers. Cost accounting includes both first-stage orders and escalations.

**Limitations.** Author-stated: confidence routing weakens on style-adversarial pairs (error-detection AUROC 0.76 versus 0.84) and fails on reference-free prose, where every judge is near chance; the comparison is not compute-matched; Jev 1.13.0 is a black box whose training data cannot be checked for benchmark exposure; human adjudication covers only 183 disagreement-selected items; a decision-only judge gives no auditable explanation; agentic use is untested. Reviewer reading: thresholds are workload- and fallback-dependent (the same rule picks τ from 0.5 to 0.9 depending on the fallback, and the GPT-5.6 Sol policy loses 4.81 points on JudgeBench), and savings shrink sharply on harder workloads. The two-order protocol doubles first-stage calls. Most cascade results are offline reconstructions from retained outputs.

**Evidence locations.** Abstract; §§3–9 and Limitations; Eq. 1; Tables 1 and 3–5; Figures 8–9; Appendices D, N–Q. Reasoning-to-Readout Self-Distillation then trains the same model without reasoning to match, at that first answer token, the average readout taken after two of its own reasoning chains; the loss adds a squared expected-level term for score questions and a KL anchor to the frozen original readout (Eq. 12). No gold labels enter the targets, although questions whose two chains disagree on the top option or are invalid are dropped. The 27B model trains on 39,279 public questions, beyond which development gains stall (Appendix F.4).

**Where it fits.** Zhuang et al. **What it contains.** *Readout.* Each typed question (choice, yes/no, or a 2–10-level ordinal score) becomes its own prompt with options lettered A–Z; probabilities are a temperature-scaled softmax over the letter logits at the last prompt position, read through the existing language-model head (Eq. 1). AutoThink (4B–27B) keeps causal attention and applies "Read-or-Think": read p₀; if its top probability reaches g = 0.9 return p₀, otherwise generate up to 4,096 reasoning tokens, read p₁ and return (1 − α)p₀ + αp₁ with α = 0.75 (Eq. 2). *Training* (§2.3). The RLCD² reward is the improvement from p₀ to p₁ in a decision-weighted proper score that emphasizes high-confidence errors (Eqs. 3–5), with both readouts taken from a frozen copy of the starting model, minus 10⁻⁴ per token beyond a soft budget (3,072 tokens for the released models); it is optimized with group-relative policy gradients (Eq. 6) on questions below the 0.9 gate only. Per-question-type temperatures are fitted on held-out training-distribution data. *Policy-aware calibration* (§5.5) refits confidence on the outputs of the deployed selection or routing policy (isotonic maps within routing-confidence strata; logistic plus isotonic for best-of-N). *Evaluation.* The public subset (20% of the score) of Decision Index (DI) 0.3 and 0.2.1, which also served as the development benchmark, and DecisionPivot, the authors' programmatically generated benchmark of 13 families × 600 items, mostly contrast pairs differing in one answer-changing detail and scored by pair accuracy; DecisionPivot is the stated independent evaluation (§4.1).

On its 7,800 core items the 27B model answers 39% directly (96% correct) and raises the remaining 61% from 57% to 93% (Figure 3). RLCD² raises 9B termination within the limit from 45.9% to 93.9% and cuts its mean trace from 2,494 to 868 tokens (−65%), with held-out default-answer accuracy unchanged within uncertainty (Table 2). The budget matters: a 512-token budget lowers 4B DecisionPivot pair accuracy from 60.4% to 52.9%, and the released 3,072-token budget still loses 4.2 points on the longest pre-RL computations at 4B (Table 3; Figure 6). Reward ablations attribute termination to the length penalty and calibration gains to the proper-score term (§3.4). **Limitations.** Author-stated: DI public scores reflect model selection and are not independent test results (§4.1); gains concentrate on task families represented in training (§5.1); restrictive reasoning budgets cost accuracy on long computations (§3.4); policy-aware calibration maps still show up to 6.1 points of overconfidence when transferred to unseen datasets (§5.5); 4-bit GGUF quantization loses up to 4.3 DecisionPivot points (Table 9). Reviewer reading: DecisionPivot is the authors' own benchmark, so the independent evaluation is still in-house, and reference scores mix public-leaderboard numbers with the authors' own runs of Decision 3.0. **What it contains.** *Interface* (§§3.2–3.3; Figure 1). Options are listed as [1] … [K] in the user turn, with thinking disabled, and the assistant turn is prefilled with "Best answer: [". Inference always uses Eq. 1; local renormalization is a training device. Full-parameter and LoRA variants are tested. *Data and protocol* (§§3.5–4.1). Training uses only public human-annotated datasets, without teacher labels, filtered by 13-gram overlap; six configurations at Qwen3.5-4B and five at Qwen3-0.6B. With image tokens prepended and no multimodal tuning, the 4B model scores 82.4% on MMBench and 64.6% on MMStar (§4.2). Decision models receive 16 rows as separate typed questions over a shared state (15-sample batches for anomaly detection); LLMs receive one row per prompt. Every request is also sent under the applicable subset of 21 information-preserving rewrites, verified by recovering the original: question rewrites (option order, names A/B or synonyms, yes/no framing, paraphrase; RQ2) and table rewrites (format such as list, sentences, JSON, Markdown; row and column order; in-context example presentation; RQ3). A decision "flips" if it differs from the original-request decision, compared against each model's identical re-send rate. Confidence is the top-option probability for decision models and ten-draw agreement for LLMs; flip AUROC measures how well low confidence predicts flips (RQ4; §§2, 6).

**Key results.** Under the original request, Jev is best on TabFact (89.7), Kev-27B on anomaly detection (81.2) and GPT-6 Luna on row classification (62.5 Macro-F1); Clef and Kev-27B beat their shared Qwen3.8-27B backbone on every task (Table 3). Jev and GPT-6 Luna Decisions take 0.08–0.11 s per row or sample, against 0.73–1.62 s for GPT-6 Luna, which costs 1.6–8× as much per decision (Table 4). From about 64 labelled rows, TabPFN-3.5 and trained models match or beat every in-context model on row classification (Figure 2). Renaming or reordering options changes 2–8% of Jev, Clef and Kev-27B classifications versus 14–30% for the open-weight LLMs, but reversing options changes 23% of GPT-6 Luna Decisions' classifications, almost all toward the option now listed first (§4; Figure 3). At least one table rewrite changes 31.7–42.8% of each model's row decisions and 17.7–63.4% of its TabFact and anomaly decisions, while aggregate scores move little (Table 5). Among a decision model's 25% most confident items, only 0.1–5% are fragile (15.5% for GPT-6 Luna Decisions on TabFact), and flip AUROC is 0.81–0.93 (Table 6; §6). Re-querying the 25% least confident rows in four other formats recovers 79–89% of the Macro-F1 gain of re-querying every row for decision models (Figure 4).

**Strengths.** Verified information-preserving rewrites separate representation from content; comparing flips with re-send rates distinguishes rewrite sensitivity from service noise. Related-backbone comparisons (Clef, Kev-27B vs Qwen3.8-27B; GPT-6 Luna through two interfaces) isolate the decision interface. Trained baselines and TabPFN mark where in-context decisions stop being competitive, and selective re-querying turns the confidence finding into a usable intervention. Exact model versions, run dates and full raw tables are given (Appendices A–C).

**Limitations.** Author-stated: one version per model, with closed services and a beta API that may change; benchmark subsets only; the 21 rewrites exclude column renaming and unit changes; decision models have shorter contexts than LLMs, limiting in-context rows. Reviewer reading: confidence tracks stability more than correctness, so stable but wrong decisions (17–23% in row classification and anomaly detection) carry high confidence and cannot be found by re-querying (Table 19); the re-querying gain mostly reflects leaving a weak original array format; Jev itself has a 4.7% re-send flip rate on row classification (Table 13). No author-linked code repository is identified in the filed paper.

**Evidence locations.** §§2–6 and 8, Limitations; Tables 1–6, 12–13 and 19; Figures 2–4; Appendices A (model versions), B (compute and run dates) and C (raw results).

## 2026-10 — Zhu et al. — Intern-Decision: Efficient Multimodal Decision Making with Autoregressive Masked Language Modeling

**Venue / provenance:** arXiv preprint `2610.22131v1`; filed PDF version dated 2026-10-15. Zotero publication date: 2026-10-15. No peer-reviewed venue recorded. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2610.22131](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2610.22131). [PDF](papers/2026/10_Zhu_et_al._Intern_Decision_Efficient_Multimodal_Decision_Making_with_Autoregressive_Masked_Language_Modeling.pdf) · [generated text](papers/text/2026/10_Zhu_et_al._Intern_Decision_Efficient_Multimodal_Decision_Making_with_Autoregressive_Masked_Language_Modeling.txt).

**Zotero citation key:** `2026.ZHU.interndecisionefficientmultimodaldecisionmakingautoregressivemaskedlanguagemodeling`

**Where it fits.** Zhu et al. (2026), *Intern-Decision: Efficient Multimodal Decision Making with Autoregressive Masked Language Modeling*, supply the technical evidence behind the separately registered [Intern-Decision-4B model card](#web-x58irxca). The model consumes a state, optional images and an ordered decision schema, and scores supplied options; it does not discover actions or learn an environment policy.

**What it contains.** A request contains a state, optional images and up to 16 typed fields (choice, yes/no or ordinal score), each option mapped to a single-token answer symbol (62 supported). The assistant turn is a complete JSON skeleton with one decision placeholder per field. Through the ordinary causal shift, the logit at the position immediately before each placeholder predicts that field's answer symbol (Eq. 1); gold answers appear only in the supervision tensor, and neither gold nor predicted answers are fed into other fields, so all fields are read from one forward pass. Later fields can attend to earlier placeholders, so the construction is not permutation-invariant. Training uses full-vocabulary cross-entropy at these shifted positions only (Eq. 2); inference normalizes only the legal answer-token logits at a checkpoint-specific temperature fitted on separate calibration data (Eq. 3). The 0.8B, 2B and 4B models start from Qwen3.5 backbones; the vision tower and projector stay frozen. All three share one training input of 17,880 samples and 23,500 decisions (5,500 with images): teacher-synthesized text decisions, public tasks (AG News, ToolACE, typed decisions, WildGuard, WildJailbreak, Ruozhiba) and synthetic game, VizDoom and pointer-action frames (§4.1; Table 10). Evaluation covers seven suites (12,351 decisions), Hard-subset probability metrics, fixed-request latency, a 96-case known-distribution pilot whose exact reference distributions come from deterministic generators, and a confidence-based handoff.

**Key results.** The 4B model reports a 90.02% unweighted seven-suite mean versus 88.74% for Jev 1.13.0, leading or tying on five suites; Jev leads WildJailBreak (Table 3). On the 111 public Jevbench-Hard items it answers 82 correctly (Jev 80) with the lowest Brier score (0.347 versus Jev's 0.358); temperature scaling lowers its ECE from 0.133 to 0.065 without changing any answer (Table 4; Figure 4). A fixed 289-token, three-field request takes 44.16 ms mean on one RTX 4090, versus 109.70 ms reported for Jev (Table 5). On the known-distribution pilot, the uncalibrated 4B model's expected Brier (0.628) is worse than Jev's (0.595), and temperature scaling brings it to 0.550, better than Jev overall and in five of six categories (Table 6). Routing 42 of the 111 Hard items to the larger Atria-Dawn-Preview raises correct answers from 82 to 97; the larger model alone scores 99, so the hybrid is two items worse at an estimated 53% less time (Table 7).

**Strengths.** Explicit next-token alignment, target-exclusion rules, checkpoint-bound temperatures and separate accuracy, latency and full-distribution evaluations make the interface auditable. The paper states its suite weighting, denominators and the paired structure of the pilot, and the public repository includes training and inference paths, scoring and deterministic pilot generation.

**Limitations.** Author-stated: the 96 pilot cases are paired order variants of 48 settings; public Jevbench informed development and is diagnostic rather than independent held-out evidence; baselines do not receive comparably refitted temperatures; the latency result is one short request shape on one device, with Jev's transport conditions undocumented; the handoff hybrid time is an estimate at one operating point; game, interface and browser demonstrations are qualitative; no matched-data ablation isolates joint field scoring or model size. Reviewer reading: Jev latency includes a network path while local latency does not, so the ratio is not a controlled speedup; external-baseline adapters are not released, so baseline rows are not reproducible from the package.

**Evidence locations.** §§3–6; Eqs. 1–3; Tables 2–7 and 10; Figures 1–4; Appendices A–C. See [Intern-Decision source map](REPOSITORIES.md#interndecisiontxt).

## 2026-10 — Chen et al. — System1-Arena: Which Decisions Can Be Left to System 1?

**Venue / provenance:** arXiv preprint `2610.21244v1`; filed PDF version dated 2026-10-15. Zotero publication date: 2026-10-15. No peer-reviewed venue recorded. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2610.21244](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2610.21244). [PDF](papers/2026/10_Chen_et_al._System1_Arena_Which_Decisions_Can_Be_Left_to_System_1.pdf) · [generated text](papers/text/2026/10_Chen_et_al._System1_Arena_Which_Decisions_Can_Be_Left_to_System_1.txt).

**Zotero citation key:** `2026.CHEN.system1arenawhichdecisionscanbeleftsystem1`

**Where it fits.** Chen, Liu, An, Wang and Zhou (2026), *System1-Arena: Which Decisions Can Be Left to System 1?*, extend the static-to-system argument of [General Decision Models](#general-decision-models) to one shared request format spanning static tasks and closed-loop environments. The question is which decisions a single-pass probability model can take over from a generative LLM, and whether accurate local readout produces successful control.

**What it contains.** Every example or environment step is a decision request: a state of named text fields plus typed questions (boolean, choice with 2–255 options, or 2–10 ordered score levels), each with instructions and option descriptions (§3.1). The static part has 92,125 questions in 105 tasks from 268 public sources: 78,362 non-agentic questions in 87 tasks (text and knowledge, applied decisions, evaluation and control) and 13,763 single-step agent, game and embodied questions. Labels are inherited from sources (test execution, solvers, experts, annotator distributions, resolved events); 33% of requests use instructions the authors wrote from source criteria (Appendix B). The closed-loop part has 138 environments from 18 families (web, text games, customer service, SWE-bench coding control, robotics, Atari, driving) run through a fixed harness that presents available actions, including candidate search terms, tool arguments or patches, as typed questions and executes the top option (§3.3). In real-time mode (Atari, Doom, driving, quadrotor), the environment advances by each model's fixed median latency with the previous action held, so slower models act on older observations (Appendix D). Thirty models are compared (§4): ten System-1 models with native probabilities (Jev 1.13, five Decision 2.0 sizes, Microsoft-Decision-1, the OpenAI Decisions API, Laya, CLM), seven closed LLMs that verbalize a JSON probability per option (normalized; five must reason at lowest effort), and thirteen open LLMs read through a softmax over answer-label token log-probabilities at the answer position, without thinking where the interface permits. Metrics are top-option accuracy, a 0–2 Brier score against hard or soft labels, median latency and cost per 1,000 requests; tasks receive equal weight.

**Key results.** Non-agentic (Table 1): Claude Opus 5.5 is most accurate (81.1%) with the lowest Brier score (0.232), at $10.38 per 1,000 requests, about 74 times GPT-6 Luna's cost. Decision-2.0-Vega-27B (73.0%, Brier 0.343) and Jev (72.6%, 0.349) beat GPT-6 Luna (71.2%, 0.461) and come within 1.4 points of the best open LLM, Gemma-4-31B (73.9%), which is less calibrated (Brier 0.474; probability ≥ 0.999 on 87% of questions). Jev costs $0.052 per 1,000 requests at 321 ms median latency, against 1.3–6.7 s for closed LLMs. The System-1 gap to the best LLM is smallest for filtering, retrieval and dialogue and largest for code and forecasting (13.3 points; Figure 3). Thinking budgets up to 2,048 tokens raise Qwen3.8-27B from 70.2% to 75.6% but worsen its Brier score from 0.391 to 0.430 (Figure 4). Agentic (Table 2): nine models reach 67.4–71.3% single-step accuracy, but closed-loop success ranges from 5.7% to 32.9%; Decision-2.0-Vega-27B, Jev and Qwen3.5-9B share 67.8% step accuracy yet succeed in 26.5%, 17.4% and 5.7% of loops. Gemma-4-31B has the highest loop success (32.9%); the four small System-1 models reach at most 3.1%. In five paired comparisons, the System-1 interface or post-training lowers agentic step accuracy in all five pairs and raises non-agentic accuracy in three (Figure 5).

**Strengths.** Identical decision requests and explicit probability scoring compare model families on the same footing, and reporting accuracy, Brier score, latency and cost together exposes ranking reversals that any single metric hides. The static/closed-loop split directly tests whether step accuracy predicts control success. Harness difficulty checks tighten environments that a random policy or most models solve too easily, and source tiers and licenses are documented per source. The [S1-Arena source](https://github.com/TianyuCodings/S1-Arena) and dataset are released.

**Limitations.** Author-stated (§7): labels and their noise are inherited, a third of instructions are author-written, many sources are public and old enough for pretraining exposure, hosted latency includes service and network time, costs reflect run-day prices, and one draw per request leaves run-to-run variation unmeasured. Reviewer reading: probability elicitation differs by family (native heads, verbalized JSON, label-token readout), so Brier comparisons partly measure the interface. Loop success averages 127 of the 138 environments (ten report only scores; one was run only with Jev), and the agentic harness itself (candidate generation, fallback actions, a scripted SWE-bench proposer that offers the reference patch) shapes what success means. Jev and some other models leave 13–14% of single-step questions unanswered, which counts as wrong. Some inputs are truncated for Laya (512 tokens) and CLM (last 2,048 tokens). Static accuracy and closed-loop success are not evidence of an optimal policy.

**Evidence locations.** §§3–5 and 7; Tables 1–3; Figures 1, 3–5; Appendices A (prompts and readouts), B (evaluation details, thinking budgets, environment counts), C (sources and tiers) and D (environments, coding controller, real-time mode). See the [System1-Arena source map](REPOSITORIES.md#system1arenatxt).
