LLM-as-Jev: LLMs Are Already Jev-Style Decision Models—When and
How to Fine-Tune Them
Yinheng Li and Justin Wagle
Jev-style decision models have gained surging traction by making decision-making dramatically faster and more efficient,
returning categorical probability distributions over predefined options without generating free-form text. However,
because Jev’s architecture and training methodologies remain proprietary, how to replicate these capabilities effectively
remains an open question. In this work, we investigate the extent to which general-purpose LLMs already possess this
capability out of the box, and when fine-tuning is actually necessary. We present LLM-as-Jev, an architecture-preserving
framework offering both a training-free inference recipe and a fine-tuning objective with KL-divergence anchors.
Across Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3-0.6B, we find that modern LLMs are inherently effective decision models: off the shelf,
frozen models match or exceed existing training-free readouts on the same 4B backbone, scale to arbitrary option
counts, and natively handle multimodal decisions. They also seamlessly inherit the backbone’s thinking mode: while
Jev was introduced as a System 1 model, “Thinking Jev” leverages budgeted test-time reasoning to boost accuracy on
challenging tasks without training. Fine-tuning provides targeted gains for smaller models and complex routing, while
our KL anchors prevent behavioral degradation in general text generation.
1. Introduction
Software systems routinely make decisions over predefined options: routing support tickets, classifying intent,
or verifying evidence rules. While chat models can perform these tasks, parsing generated text introduces
latency and formatting failures. Jev [Almeida, 2026] addresses this by directly returning a probability distribution
across candidate options without free-form generation. Jev aims to be fast and cost-effective while retaining
LLM versatility: deploying a new task requires only a prompt and option list rather than a dedicated classifier.
Although Jev’s weights remain proprietary, the public JevBench leaderboard [Benchmark Heaven, 2026] already
lists over 60 community Jev-style models.
A central design choice among community Jev-style models is whether to update model parameters. Many
approaches fine-tune the backbone with adapters or classification heads; others evaluate frozen LLMs directly.
Surprisingly, fine-tuning does not guarantee superior performance: for instance, reflex 4B [kshetrajna12, 2026]
achieves 79.7% accuracy on public JevBench items, slightly trailing SemIf [Lee, 2026], which simply extracts
option-letter logits from the unmodified frozen model (80.5%; Table 1). This raises two fundamental questions:
to what extent do pre-trained LLMs inherently possess decision-making capabilities, and under what conditions
does fine-tuning yield practical gains?
Our approach: leveraging off-the-shelf LLMs.We present a framework that turns existing causal LLMs
into Jev-style decision models without altering their architectures, tokenizers, or vocabularies, while minimizing
dependence on task-specific training. In contrast to many community Jev-style models that introduce custom
classification heads or reserved tokens [Palmer, 2026, mohit67890, 2026, FlyMy.AI, 2026]—which must be
retrained for every new backbone—our architecture-preserving design provides three distinct benefits:

2. Background and Related Work
Decision models, Jev, and JevBench.Traditionally, classification tasks relied on discriminative models
like BERT [Devlin et al., 2019], which require dedicated heads and supervised fine-tuning over fixed label
sets. In contrast, Jev [Almeida, 2026] functions like a general LLM: it evaluates arbitrary tasks zero-shot
through prompting, directly emitting categorical probability distributions over discrete candidates without text
generation. Jev formalizes decisions across three formats:choice( 𝐾 options),noul(binary verification), and
score(ordinal grading). To assess such systems, JevBench [Benchmark Heaven, 2026] benchmarks decision
models across intelligence, calibration, latency, and cost across 231 public items (easy, standard, hard), while
sealed splits remain private.
Community Jev-style models.Table 15 (Appendix J) summarizes community efforts, which broadly fall
into two paradigms:Frozen-model readoutsextract predictions from output logits without weight updates, using
option-letter logits [Lee, 2026, IkerMoel, 2026], per-option querying [Yinsongxu, 2026], label-prior and option-
order correction [Zhang et al., 2026], or temperature-scaled letter logits on larger backbones [blockbrain-ai, 2026,
Octalab, 2026]. In contrast, LLM-as-Jev jointly scores arbitrary candidates in a single prompt (§3.3), matching
or outperforming the same-backbone frozen readouts in Table 15 (§4.2).Fine-tuned readoutsadapt backbones
via LoRA or full tuning on synthetic or replayed data [allebee, 2026, crh225, 2026, HopitAI, 2026, kshetrajna12,
2026, Mapika, 2026]. However, they typically rely on costly teacher-generated data and risk representation drift,
whereas our anchored objective preserves general model capabilities.
Probabilistic calibration.In automated decision systems, well-calibrated probabilities are as vital as top-1
accuracy, allowing downstream workflows to defer low-confidence predictions to human review. Because
modern neural networks can be poorly calibrated [Guo et al., 2017] and LLM calibration varies with scale and
fine-tuning [Desai and Durrett, 2020, Kadavath et al., 2022, Tian et al., 2023], we evaluate decision reliability
using Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score directly on raw model distributions without post-hoc
temperature scaling.
3. The LLM-as-Jev Framework
3.1. Problem Formulation
A decision instance consists of a context state 𝑠, a set of candidate options 𝑜1, . . . , 𝑜𝐾, and a target choice
𝑦∈ {1, . . . , 𝐾} . The objective is to produce a probability distribution 𝑃(𝑘|𝑠) over the candidate set. All three
Jev-supported task types map cleanly into this formulation: a verification (yes/no) query specifies two options
describing the acceptance criteria for “yes” and “no”, while an ordinal scoring query defines an ordered sequence
of graded options.

3.2. Answer Interface
We query the model using its default chat template with thinking mode disabled, formatting the user prompt as
follows:
State: {𝑠}
Options: [1] {𝑜 1} ... [K] {𝑜 𝐾}
Select one option. Answer only with its bracketed numeric identifier.
Each candidate is rendered on a separate line. As shown in Figure 1(a), we condition the assistant’s turn on
the fixed prefix Best answer: [ , such that selecting option 𝑘 corresponds to generating the completion
“𝑘]”. We define this completion as thecandidate suffix(Figure 1b), represented by the token sequence 𝑐𝑘 =
(𝑐𝑘,1, . . . , 𝑐𝑘,𝑛𝑘). This design yields three key advantages:
• Scaling to arbitrary options.Because every candidate suffix terminates with a closing bracket, no
complete suffix can serve as the prefix of another (e.g.,1] is not a prefix of 12]). This prefix-free property
supports arbitrarily large candidate sets 𝐾, unlike single-letter readouts that are inherently bounded to
16 or 26 options.
• Fast parallel evaluation.The candidate suffixes naturally form a shallow prefix tree (Figure 1b) whose
depth is governed by the underlying tokenizer. Tokenizers that process digits individually (such as Qwen’s)
yield a trie depth of at most ⌊log10 𝐾⌋+ 2 , while multi-digit tokenizers require depth two, enabling rapid
parallel scoring across all branches (Appendix C).
• Preserving text generation.The model retains the ability to emit structured answers in standard
autoregressive text generation. We utilize this text mode to verify that the model adheres to the expected
syntax post-training; fine-tuning leaves general generation capabilities intact (§4.3).

Joint contextual scoring.Conditioning suffix evaluations on the entire prompt allows the model to jointly
evaluate all alternatives. In contrast, several community models score options in isolation [Kwok et al., 2026] or
via separate binary prompts [Yinsongxu, 2026]. However, isolated evaluation struggles on queries requiring
comparative judgment (such as finding the closest semantic match or “none of the above”); on JevBench, isolated
scoring trails joint readouts by about 5 points on identical backbones (76.2% for LLM2Jev vs. 81.4%; Table 15).
While joint scoring introduces minor susceptibility to option order [Zheng et al., 2024], we mitigate position
bias during fine-tuning via random option shuffling.
Parallel suffix scoring.Because candidate suffixes are fixed and known in advance, they are scored con-
currently via teacher forcing after caching the KV activations of 𝑥. As illustrated in Figure 2, even for 150+
candidates (e.g., CLINC150), evaluation requires only a single prefill and at most 1–3 brief forward steps with-
out autoregressive decoding, drastically outperforming isolated 𝒪(𝐾) scoring [Kwok et al., 2026, jev-local
contributors, 2026].

3.5. Training Data Construction
We curate training sets exclusively from publicly accessible, human-annotated datasets, converting each source
into our unified decision template without altering ground-truth labels or soliciting synthetic labels from teacher
models. To prevent train-test contamination, we filter all training instances that share a 13-word n-gram (or
an identical sentence of six or more words) with any evaluation benchmark, and purge exact duplicates of
validation inputs.
Training mixtures were developed iteratively: we began with canonical intent-classification tasks highlighted
in early Jev demonstrations [Almeida, 2026], subsequently incorporating reasoning and long-context corpora
to expand coverage. Because subsequent data iterations were motivated by error analysis on public JevBench
samples, we treat the evaluations in §4.3 as exploratory; JevBench was strictly excluded from hyperparameter
tuning and checkpoint selection. Appendix G details each data mixture, and Appendix D provides complete
dataset breakdowns.
4. Experiments
We outline the experimental setup in §4.1, followed by our core empirical findings: demonstrating that frozen
LLMs are effective decision models out of the box (§4.2), delineating the conditions under which fine-tuning
provides tangible benefits (§4.3), and establishing optimal fine-tuning and regularization practices (§4.4).
4.1. Setup
Foundation models.We conduct experiments on Qwen3.5-4B [Qwen Team, 2026] (with thinking disabled
except in §4.5) and the compact Qwen3-0.6B [Yang et al., 2025]. This choice aligns with prevalent community
Jev-style models that build upon the same backbones [Lee, 2026, crh225, 2026, HopitAI, 2026, kshetrajna12, 2026,
allebee, 2026, IkerMoel, 2026], facilitating direct and fair comparisons. Furthermore, a dense 4B model incurs an
inference cost of $0.046/MTok (Table 17, Appendix L), closely matching Jev’s official pricing ($0.042/MTok) and
establishing a realistic baseline for fast System 1 decision-making.
Training configurations.We evaluate six training configurations at 4B and five at 0.6B, spanning varying
data mixtures and step budgets (§4.3; complete dataset compositions and step budgets in Tables 3 and 7), all
trained under the unified objective in §3.4. In §4.4, we retrain Reason+Long-12k across different anchor weights
under both full-parameter tuning and LoRA.
Evaluation dimensions.We evaluate models across five complementary dimensions (full dataset specifi-
cations, splits, and citations in Appendix B and Table 4): (1)External benchmarks: seven classification and
reasoning datasets retaining full candidate sets (BoolQ, MMLU, MMLU-Pro, ARC-C, WinoGrande, SciQ, and
77-way Banking77); (2)JevBench: 231 public items as an out-of-distribution diagnostic suite; (3)General
capabilities: GSM8K, IFEval, TriviaQA, LAMBADA, and WikiText-2; (4)Multimodal perception: MMBench
and MMStar; and (5)Conversational behavior: generation stability on Dolly-15k and MT-Bench.
Metrics and significance testing.We evaluate accuracy, ECE, and the Brier score (§2). Model comparisons
are validated using two-sided exact sign tests over the subset of instances on which predictions diverge.

4.2. An LLM Is Already a Decision Model
Strong zero-shot decision capability at 4B.Without task-specific adaptation, frozen Qwen3.5-4B achieves
81.4% on public JevBench (188/231 correct; Table 1), on par with the training-free SemIf (80.5%) and above the
fine-tuned reflex 4B (79.7%) on the same backbone. On External benchmarks, LLM-as-Jev outperforms SemIf by
3.2 percentage points in macro accuracy (313 wins vs. 154 losses), driven by solid gains on MMLU, MMLU-Pro,
and ARC-C. Because both methods evaluate the exact same weights, these improvements validate the power of
joint candidate conditioning and prefix-free scoring.
Effortless scaling to high-cardinality tasks.Many real-world tasks involve dozens or hundreds of can-
didates (e.g., Banking77 with 77 intents, CLINC150 with 151 classes). Here, letter-based readouts fail due to
fixed 26-option limits, while isolated scoring models require dozens of separate forward passes. In contrast,
LLM-as-Jev evaluates all candidates jointly in a single pass, achieving 69.0% zero-shot accuracy on Banking77
with minimal overhead.
Calibrated probabilities without post-hoc scaling.The training-free 4B model achieves an ECE of 0.057
on JevBench out of the box, outperforming both SemIf (0.061) and reflex 4B (0.070, even with its post-hoc
calibration mapping), confirming that raw next-token probabilities over numeric suffixes remain well-calibrated.

4.3. When Fine-Tuning Helps
To determine when parameter adaptation genuinely benefits decision models, we evaluate six training config-
urations at 4B and five at 0.6B (Table 7). As summarized in Figure 4, our empirical findings reveal three key
principles:
Gains concentrate strictly on seen task domains.Performance improvements track the specific task
structures covered during training, with little cross-domain transfer to unrepresented domains (Figure 4).
Because every mixture incorporates intent classification, intent routing on Banking77 improves consistently
across all configurations, gaining 4.9–7.7 points at 4B and 37–42 points at 0.6B. Conversely, knowledge-intensive
benchmarks absent from the training mixtures, such as MMLU and TriviaQA, remain virtually flat regardless
of training scale. Fine-tuning aligns output format and candidate discrimination, but cannot inject world
knowledge that the pre-trained backbone lacks.
Format diversity and length alignment prevent heuristic shortcuts.Data composition and length
diversity govern transfer fidelity far more than training volume alone:
• The Intent Trap: Training solely on intent routing induces severe inductive bias: the model develops an
aggressive confirmation bias toward “yes”, with affirmative predictions surging on binary queries and
suppressing fallback options, which degrades overall JevBench accuracy from 81.4% to 74.9%.
• The Short-Context Trap: Incorporating balanced reasoning corpora eliminates this affirmative bias and
restores baseline accuracy. However, scaling short-context supervision alone impairs long-document
comprehension, with correct predictions on long items dropping from 23 to 17.

ance, substantially boosting high-cardinality routing on Banking77 from 22.2% to 60.5% and improving
calibration.
• Capable models (4B)already exhibit strong zero-shot decision boundaries and calibration. Consequently,
full fine-tuning yields diminishing returns on broad out-of-distribution suites like JevBench, with gains
restricted to targeted enterprise domains. Only parameter-efficient adaptation via LoRA improves overall
accuracy (§4.4).
Importantly, fine-tuning at both scales preserves general linguistic capabilities and potentially other task
capabilities (Appendix G), while 4B models fully retain zero-shot visual decisions on MMBench (Table 10).
4.4. How to Fine-Tune
Deploying decision tuning in practice involves two critical choices: selecting the regularization strength 𝜆
and deciding between full-parameter tuning and parameter-efficient adapters. We evaluate these trade-offs by
retraining Reason+Long-12k across𝜆∈ {1,0.1,0.01,0}under both regimes (Figure 5; Table 11).
Benchmark scores conceal generative collapse.Standard benchmark scores remain deceptively flat
across anchor weights (Figure 5, left). However, omitting anchors (𝜆= 0 ) severely destabilizes autoregressive
generation: unanchored 4B compulsively emits trailing text after closing brackets in 69 of 231 JevBench queries,

format-breaking generations (none at 4B, one at 0.6B) while maintaining JevBench accuracy.

While original Jev operates as an intuitive “System 1” model without intermediate reasoning, most remaining

gains concentrate on hard multi-hop questions, and the final readout consistently selects the option reached by
the thought.

strictly budgeted: buying frontier accuracy on hard decisions while remaining over 5× cheaper than large
models in standard non-thinking mode (Appendix L).

5. Conclusion
We presented LLM-as-Jev, an architecture-preserving framework turning off-the-shelf causal LLMs into cal-
ibrated Jev-style decision models through prefix-free conditioning, with or without parameter adaptation.
Modern LLMs are inherently strong decision models: frozen backbones match or exceed existing training-
free readouts of the same model, scale to arbitrary options, handle multimodal inputs, and unlock deliberate
reasoning via Thinking Jev.
For practical deployment, we synthesize three core principles: (1)Default to zero-shot: foundation models
provide robust baselines out of the box, with Thinking Jev scaling reasoning on demand; (2)Aligned adaptation:
fine-tuning is most valuable for compact models or specialized routing when training distributions match test
formats and lengths; and (3)Anchor with LoRA: pairing parameter-efficient LoRA with KL anchors ( 𝜆= 1 )
maximizes non-thinking 4B accuracy while preventing generative collapse. Together, these results establish a
reliable, lightweight bridge between generative foundation models and deterministic decision systems.

A. Implementation Details
Prompt.The full user-message template is:
State: {state}
Options:
[1] {option 1}
[2] {option 2}
...
Select one option. Answer only with its bracketed numeric identifier.
We apply the model’s chat template withadd_generation_prompt=Trueand
enable_thinking=False.
• Scoring modeappends Best answer: [ to the formatted prompt and computes the log-probability
of each candidate suffix “𝑘]”.
• Text modecontinues the same prompt, including Best answer: [ , with greedy generation for up
to 32 new tokens and stops at EOS. The concatenated prefix and generated completion must begin with a
valid candidate identifier [𝑘], which is extracted as the predicted answer; any trailing text is discarded,
including output truncated at the length limit. Generations failing this leading syntax count as parse
failures.
We take each candidate’s suffix tokens from the tokenization of the full string, the prompt followed by the suffix,
and check that the prompt’s tokens form a prefix of it, so all candidates share one prompt prefill (Appendix C).
Reference statistics.Before training, we use the frozen original model to compute and cache the following
anchor statistics for each example:
•the legal mass at each anchored node;
•the top-64 illegal tokens and the mass of the remaining illegal tokens;
•the top-64 tokens and the remaining mass at each anchored position.
Training reads these cached statistics, so it does not need a second model in memory. This top-𝑘 truncation
substantially compresses cache storage: retaining only the 64 most probable tokens and a single remainder
bucket per anchored site requires negligible footprint, compared to storing full vocabulary distributions (151,936
tokens for Qwen3-0.6B; 248,320 for Qwen3.5-4B). Appendix I provides systematic ablations over anchor sites,
top-64 truncation, and off-path node sampling.
Latency.Our reference implementation uses FP32 and is not optimized for speed. On a single RTX A5500,
median latency is 194 ms for 4B and 76 ms for 0.6B; SemIf takes 46 ms in BF16. These measurements are
indicative, and latency optimization is outside the scope of this work.

C. Tokenizer Differences
The LLM-as-Jev interface imposes only one formal invariant: the token sequence of the prompt prefix must
strictly form a prefix of the full tokenization for each completed candidate. The structural topology of the
candidate trie, however, depends on how the underlying tokenizer segments numeric strings. We systematically
evaluated three widely used tokenizer families across a 1,000-option prompt (Figure 7); all three yield structurally
prefix-free tries for identifiers 1 through 1,000.

Digit grouping.Qwen and Llama 2 tokenize digits individually, so an identifier with 𝑑 digits takes 𝑑+ 1
tokens, and the trie has depth ⌊log10 𝐾⌋+ 2 . Tokenizers that group up to three digits, such as those of Phi-4
and Phi-4-mini, make every identifier up to 999 a single token followed by the closing bracket, so the trie has
depth two. Scoring then needs one short forward step after the prompt prefill instead of up to three, and the
tree-factorized loss reduces to a flat cross-entropy over the legal first tokens. Beyond 999, identifiers span several
tokens again (1000 becomes 100 0 ), but the closing bracket keeps the suffixes prefix-free, so the number of
options remains unlimited.
Word-start markers.SentencePiece tokenizers such as that of Llama 2 treat the start of a string as the start
of a word. Tokenized on its own, the suffix 1] therefore gains a word-start token that it never has in context,
where it follows the opening bracket. We take each suffix’s tokens from the tokenization of the full string
instead and check that the prompt’s tokens form a prefix of it. The prompt tokens are then identical across
candidates, so parallel decoding with one shared prompt prefill is unaffected. A tokenizer that merged the
prompt’s final token with the start of a suffix would fail this check; ending the prefill with an opening bracket
avoids such merges for all tokenizers we checked. For Qwen, taking suffix tokens from the full string or from
the suffix alone gives identical results.
Isolating the option-10 effect.With tokenizers that split digits individually, the suffixes of options 1 and 10
share their first token, and the next node chooses between the closing bracket and 0. Tokenizers that group
digits remove this shared node, so they offer a way to isolate the option-10 effect described in Appendix H.

I. Anchor Sites and Sizes
Setup.To test whether each anchor site is needed, we retrain Reason+Long-12k with 𝜆= 1 and all parameters
at both scales, removing one part of the anchors at a time (Table 14): the two context positions, the off-path
nodes (so that only the gold path is anchored), or the two distribution anchors ℒout and ℒpos, which leaves the
legal mass alone and is equivalent to keeping 𝑘= 0 tokens. To size the truncation and the number of off-path
nodes without training more models, we compare the original model with the trained models on the first 64
development examples of each of the 12 training datasets (theanalysis examples), at the sites that training
anchors; there we sample eight off-path nodes per example instead of three.
Every anchor site serves a distinct regularizing role.While no single site ablation significantly degrades
JevBench accuracy (𝑝≥0.38), each omission directly destabilizes the specific distribution it targets. Omitting
context positions inflates KL divergence at the reply start by 9.4×at 4B and 12.6×at 0.6B (and 2.4×and 2.6×
after the bracket); consequently, three text-mode responses emit runaway text at 4B and three reach the length
limit at 0.6B, while JevBench ECE rises from 0.070 to 0.089 at 4B and from 0.118 to 0.143 at 0.6B. Removing
off-path node anchors increases KL divergence over illegal tokens at unvisited branches by 2.9× at 4B (0.084
to 0.241) and 1.8× at 0.6B (0.176 to 0.319), elevating 0.6B ECE to 0.165. Constraining legal mass alone fails to

prevent generative runaway at both scales (16 4B completions append trailing text and 39 0.6B completions hit
the length cap), causing reply-start KL to surge 95-fold at 4B and 40-fold at 0.6B—comparable to unconstrained
training without any anchors (44- and 51-fold). In conversational chat, omitting off-path nodes or isolating legal
mass induces significantly more non-terminating replies than the base model at 0.6B. Variations on Banking77
remain bounded within ±1.8 points; given single-seed runs, we do not attribute these modest shifts to individual

Top-64 truncation captures the distribution tail.At candidate trie nodes, the base model’s 64 most
probable illegal tokens account on average for 94.5–98.9% of total illegal probability mass (rising to 97.1–99.6%
with 1,024 tokens), while at context positions the top-64 tokens cover over 99.9% of next-token probability. By the
chain rule of KL divergence, the true divergence exceeds our truncated, bucketed objective only by the product
of the base model’s tail mass and the residual divergence within the tail; the bound is therefore exceptionally
tight when tail mass is light. Across anchored models, this truncation gap averages at most 7.3×10 −4 nats per
anchored site (1.9×10 −3 without anchors). Moreover, caching truncated distributions consumes only ≈0.5 KB
per distribution, compared to 0.6 MB (Qwen3-0.6B) or 1.0 MB (Qwen3.5-4B) for the full vocabulary in FP32.
Truncating aggressively to 𝑘= 0 completely removes distributional constraints, reproducing the degenerate
behavior observed in legal-mass-only runs.
Sampling three off-path nodes is computationally optimal.Because a four-option query contains exactly
three off-path candidate nodes, setting the sample size to three provides exhaustive off-path coverage for 9 of
the 12 training datasets in Reason+Long-12k; only the three intent datasets (spanning 27–151 options) require
stochastic subsampling across iterations. Regularizing three randomly sampled nodes per training instance
maintains all off-path branches close to the base model: on held-out analysis instances where eight off-path
nodes are evaluated per intent query, off-path KL divergence (0.084 at 4B, 0.176 at 0.6B) remains virtually
identical to gold-path divergence (0.089 and 0.192), where every node is anchored deterministically. Increasing
the number of sampled nodes yields diminishing returns while imposing substantial computational overhead,
as each anchored node requires an additional forward evaluation over the prompt prefix. Sampling three nodes
increases evaluated forward tokens 3.4-fold per instance, resulting in a 2.25× slowdown at 4B and 2.23× at
0.6B relative to unanchored training.

L. Inference Cost and Latency Details
Measured latency and price depend on the serving stack: hardware, precision, batching, and caching. We
therefore count quantities that do not, the floating-point operations (FLOPs) and the sequential forward passes
per decision, and we convert FLOPs into prices with models whose price and size are both public (Table 17).
FLOPs per token.A forward pass costs about two FLOPs per parameter and token [Kaplan et al., 2020].
For each text token, Qwen3.5-4B runs its 3.57B decoder parameters; its tied embedding matrix acts as the
output layer only where logits are needed, and its vision encoder and multi-token-prediction head stay idle. At
JevBench’s prompt lengths, attention in its 8 full-attention layers and the state updates of its 24 linear-attention

layers add 3%, for 7.36 GFLOPs per input token. Qwen3-0.6B spends 0.88 GFLOPs in its linear layers and 0.23
GFLOPs in attention at JevBench’s lengths, plus 0.31 GFLOPs in its output layer at each position with logits.
From FLOPs to price.Jev’s announcement prices the input of existing LLMs at $0.20 to $10 per million tokens
(MTok), with output about five times more expensive, and Jev’s input at $0.042/MTok with free output [Almeida,
2026]. Open-weight models are sold today at the low end of this range. Fireworks charges $0.20/MTok for any
dense model with 4B–16B parameters that it does not list individually, the largest of which need 32 GFLOPs per
token [Fireworks AI, 2026], and Together AI charges $0.20/MTok of input for Qwen3-235B-A22B, which activates
22B parameters, or 44 GFLOPs per token [Together AI, 2026a]. At the same price per FLOP, Qwen3.5-4B’s
input would cost $0.046/MTok against the dense reference and $0.034/MTok against the sparse one, which
brackets Jev’s $0.042. In fact, Together AI already sells a decision model fine-tuned from Qwen3.5-4B, Tev1 4B
Experimental, at $0.04/MTok of input with free output [Together AI, 2026b]. We use the more conservative
$0.046/MTok below.
The readout’s output is nearly free.Like Jev, the readout generates no text. After the prompt’s prefill, one
batched step scores the closing bracket of every candidate suffix (JevBench items have at most six options),
which adds 2.3% to the prompt’s FLOPs on average. A decision thus costs about as much as its input: $30 per
million JevBench decisions, against $27 at Jev’s price and $2,477 for GPT-5.6 Sol at its API price on 2026-10-12, a
promotional price offered at least through 2026-11-21 [OpenAI, 2026]. Fine-tuning does not change these costs,
since the fine-tuned models keep the architecture and LoRA weights are merged.
Thinking is priced as output.Thinking tokens are decoded one at a time, a regime limited by memory
bandwidth rather than compute [Pope et al., 2023], which is why APIs charge more for output; we price them at
five times the input price, the ratio Jev’s announcement gives for existing LLMs. At budgets of 1,024 to 4,096
tokens, thinking multiplies the FLOPs of a decision by 2.3 to 4.4 but its price by 6.5 to 15, since its 711 to 1,822
thinking tokens on average are billed as output. It also replaces the readout’s two sequential forward passes
with 713 to 1,824, so its latency grows with the thought on any hardware.
When to think.With a 4,096-token budget, the 4B model reaches GPT-5.6 Sol’s accuracy (94.8% vs. 94.4%) at
an estimated $450 per million decisions, 5.5 times less than GPT-5.6 Sol, which we query without reasoning.
Thinking Jev therefore empowers decisions where accuracy is paramount (deliberative System 2), while the
default readout preserves a Jev-like price ($30 per million decisions) and two sequential forward passes for
high-volume, real-time System 1 pipelines.
Sensitivity.The price per FLOP varies across providers and models. Against the sparse reference, all of our
prices fall by 27%, to $22 per million decisions for the readout and $327 at a 4,096-token budget. Pricing output
at four times input, as both providers do for gpt-oss-120B, lowers the cost of thinking to $162 at 1,024 tokens and
$366 at 4,096. Size-tiered list prices charge small models more per FLOP: at Fireworks’ $0.10 or $0.20/MTok for
models below or above 4B parameters, the readout would cost $65 or $131 per million decisions. The latencies
we measured (Appendix A) come from an unoptimized FP32 implementation and do not enter these estimates.
