StartLux-Decision: AutoThink Decision Models with
Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Deliberative Decisions

Abstract:We introduce StartLux-Decision, state-of-the-art decision models spanning 0.8B–27B parameters, com-
bining efficient single-pass prediction with adaptive, confidence-guided reasoning. TheInstantmodels leverage
bidirectional attention in full-attention layers to improve decision accuracy while preserving efficient single-pass
inference. TheAutoThinkmodels selectively invoke explicit reasoning for uncertain decisions while answering
confident cases directly. We develop a three-stage training pipeline that establishes shared decision capabilities,
specializes the two variants, and equips AutoThink with explicit reasoning through reasoning supervision and
reinforcement learning. To improve reasoning efficiency while preserving decision quality, we introduce Rein-
forcement Learning for Calibrated Deliberative Decisions (RLCD 2). Extending decision-oriented reinforcement
learning to explicit reasoning, RLCD 2 uses a frozen model and proper scoring rules to reward improvements in
decision probability quality, while imposing a soft penalty on overlong reasoning traces. RLCD 2 increases the
proportion of reasoning traces that terminate within the generation limit by up to 48 percentage points and reduces
average reasoning length by up to 65%, while largely preserving decision accuracy. On the public subset of Decision
Index 0.3, the Instant models outperform the strongest evaluated comparable-scale open-weight decision models by
7.4–16.9 points, with the 27B AutoThink model further improving its score to 73.4. We also introduce DecisionPivot,
a programmatically verifiable benchmark of computational decisions featuring contrast pairs that differ in a single
answer-changing detail. On DecisionPivot, AutoThink consistently outperforms its Instant counterparts by 11.5–18.4
percentage points in pair accuracy and achieves leading performance among evaluated systems, highlighting
the benefits of adaptive reasoning for computation-intensive decisions. To facilitate practical deployment, we
support inference across vLLM, llama.cpp, and MLX, together with quantized model variants for reduced memory
requirements. We envision StartLux-Decision advancing a new generation of decision models that adaptively
allocate reasoning to challenging decisions, balancing decision quality, computational efficiency, and reliability.

StartLux-Decision Introduction
1. Introduction
Decision-making is central to modern large language model (LLM) systems, underpinning tasks such as request
routing, retrieval assessment, tool-call validation, and agent outcome verification (Gu et al., 2024). Adecision
modelsupports these operations by answering typed questions about an input state and producing probability
distributions over candidate options (Tang and Zheng, 2026). Beyond identifying the most likely outcome, these
probabilities enable downstream systems to decide whether to act autonomously, invoke a stronger model, or defer
to human review. Existing open-weight decision models largely rely on single-pass prediction without explicit
reasoning (Cloudflare, 2026; Perplexity AI, 2026). While efficient, this approach can struggle with computation-
intensive decisions involving arithmetic, scheduling, and procedural verification, where multi-step reasoning may be
essential (Wei et al., 2022; Snell et al., 2024). Moreover, we observe that decision-oriented training can substantially
impair a model’s ability to generate and reliably terminate reasoning traces. Enabling token generation alone
is insufficient to recover effective reasoning. These challenges raise a central question:How can decision models
incorporate reliable reasoning when needed while retaining efficient single-pass prediction and probabilistic decision outputs?
To address this challenge, we introduce StartLux-Decision, a family of decision models spanning 0.8B–27B parame-
ters, with two complementary variants sharing a probability interface. TheInstantmodels leverage bidirectional
attention in full-attention layers to improve decision accuracy while preserving efficient single-pass inference. This
allows earlier prompt representations to incorporate information from subsequent questions and candidate options.
TheAutoThinkmodels selectively invoke explicit reasoning for uncertain decisions while answering confident cases
directly, combining initial and post-reasoning probabilities when needed. A three-stage training pipeline establishes
shared decision capabilities, specializes the two variants, and equips AutoThink with explicit reasoning through
reasoning supervision and reinforcement learning (Figure 2; Section 2).
We introduce Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Deliberative Decisions (RLCD 2), building on Reinforcement
Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD) (Almeida, 2026) to extend decision-oriented reinforcement learning
to explicit reasoning. Rather than rewarding answer correctness alone, RLCD 2 optimizes the reasoning policy
for the quality of the probabilistic decisions it supports. A frozen copy of the starting model provides decision
probability readouts before and after reasoning, with their proper-score difference defining the decision-quality
reward. A soft length penalty further discourages unnecessarily long reasoning traces, balancing decision quality
with computational efficiency. Empirically, RLCD2 increases the proportion of reasoning traces terminating within
the generation limit by up to 48 percentage points and reduces average reasoning length by up to 65%, while largely
preserving decision accuracy (Section 3).
StartLux-Decision demonstrates strong performance across established decision benchmarks. On the public subset
of Decision Index 0.3, the Instant models outperform the strongest evaluated comparable-scale open-weight decision
models by 7.4–16.9 points, with the 4B Instant model surpassing all evaluated reference decision models (Figure 1).
Aggregate benchmark scores, however, can obscure sensitivity to small but decision-critical input changes. We
therefore introduce DecisionPivot (StartLux, 2026), a programmatically verifiable benchmark of computational
decisions featuring contrast pairs that differ in a single answer-changing detail. By requiring both decisions in each
contrast pair to be correct, DecisionPivot evaluates whether models can reliably distinguish between closely related
cases with different correct answers. On DecisionPivot, AutoThink consistently outperforms its Instant counterparts
by 11.5–18.4 percentage points in pair accuracy, with the 27B AutoThink model reaching 86.9% and leading all
evaluated reference systems (Section 4).
Beyond predictive accuracy, the reliability of decision probabilities also depends on how downstream systems
select and route predictions. Calibration of individual predictions does not necessarily carry over to these
downstream policies. For example, predictions assigned 80–90% confidence by our 9B verifier achieve 92% accuracy
when evaluated individually, but only 78% after selecting the highest-scoring response from 32 candidates. This
discrepancy reveals how downstream selection can distort confidence–accuracy relationships even when the
underlying model remains unchanged. We introduce policy-aware calibration, which incorporates selection and
routing information to calibrate confidence under the deployed policy, reducing confidence–accuracy gaps to within
2.7 percentage points across 184 settings spanning four decision pipelines (Section 5). For practical deployment,
we support both variants across vLLM, llama.cpp, and MLX. We also provide quantized model variants to reduce
memory requirements and facilitate deployment across diverse hardware environments (Section 6).

StartLux-Decision Model Design and Training
Together, these contributions establish StartLux-Decision as a state-of-the-art decision model family that combines
strong predictive performance with efficient single-pass inference and adaptive reasoning through a shared
probabilistic decision interface. More broadly, we envision a new generation of decision models that not only make
accurate predictions but also determine when additional reasoning is warranted and how confidently their outputs
should guide downstream actions, balancing decision quality, computational efficiency, and reliability. We publicly
release the model weights and inference code to enable independent evaluation, facilitate practical deployment, and
advance further research on adaptive and reliable decision-making. Our implementation and deployment resources
are available athttps://github.com/StartLuxLabs/StartLux-Decision.
2. Model Design and Training
StartLux-Decision comprises two complementary variants, Instant and AutoThink, designed for efficient single-pass
prediction and adaptive reasoning, respectively. We develop a novel three-stage training pipeline that establishes
foundational decision capabilities, specializes the two variants through task and attention adaptation, and equips
AutoThink with explicit reasoning through reasoning supervision and reinforcement learning.
2.1 Decision Formulation and Probability Readout
Existing open-weight decision models typically answer structured questions about an input state by assigning
probabilities to candidate options, often through a single forward pass (Tang and Zheng, 2026; Cloudflare, 2026;
Perplexity AI, 2026).
Typed decisions.A decision request comprises an input state and a set of named, typed questions concerning that
state. The input state may be represented as a string or any JSON value, optionally accompanied by images. Each
question specifies a choice among named options, a binary decision, or a level on an ordered scale with 2 to 10
levels. Candidate options may include natural-language criteria that further define their intended semantics. For
each question, the model produces a probability distribution over candidate options, providing both a predicted
outcome and a confidence estimate to guide downstream decisions.
One-pass readout.Existing open-weight decision models typically produce probability distributions over candidate
options through a single forward pass, without autoregressive token generation. These probabilities can be obtained
through dedicated prediction heads or directly from the output logits of a pretrained language model. In the latter
approach, each question is formatted as a prompt containing the input state, the question, and candidate options
mapped to output tokens, whose logits are read from the language-model head at the final prompt position. For a
question with K candidate options, let zk denote the logit associated with option k. The decision probabilities can
then be obtained by applying temperature scaling and a softmax over the candidate options:

2.2 Model Variants and Adaptive Inference
Shared probability readout.StartLux-Decision adopts an option-letter probability readout that maps each decision
question to a separate prompt containing the input state, question, and candidate options labeled with single letters
(A–Z). The corresponding logits are extracted at the final prompt position through the existing language-model
output projection, without introducing an additional prediction head. The same readout is used for Instant’s
single-pass predictions and AutoThink’s initial and post-reasoning probability estimates. Each readout supports
up to 26 candidate options. Longer lists are processed in parallel groups of at most 25 options, with the top three
candidates from each group advancing to a final round (Appendix D.4). Questions within a request are processed
as separate batch rows. Both variants support contexts of up to 262,144 tokens and process images through the
pretrained vision tower, which remains frozen during training.

Instant models.TheInstantmodels follow the single-pass decision paradigm, producing decision probabilities
without autoregressive token generation. Since the complete prompt is available before prediction, we enable
bidirectional attention in the full-attention layers, allowing earlier prompt representations to incorporate information
from subsequent questions and candidate options. The linear-attention layers remain causal, preserving the
underlying hybrid architecture while adapting full-attention computation to the single-pass decision setting.
AutoThink models and the default answer.TheAutoThinkmodels extend single-pass prediction throughRead-or-
Think, a confidence-guided inference mechanism that selectively invokes explicit reasoning for uncertain decisions
while answering confident cases directly. For each question, AutoThink first performs a causal one-pass readout to
obtain an initial probability distribution p0 over candidate options. If the maximum predicted probability is at least
the confidence threshold g= 0.9, the model returns p0 without additional reasoning. Otherwise, it reprocesses the
prompt with reasoning enabled, generates a reasoning trace of up to 4,096 tokens, and obtains a post-reasoning
distribution p1 from the option-letter logits following the trace. The reasoning process and both probability readouts
share the same model weights. When reasoning is invoked, AutoThink combines the initial and post-reasoning
probability distributions to form its final prediction, rather than relying exclusively on the post-reasoning estimate.

2.3 Three-Stage Training Pipeline
Our three-stage training pipeline develops the complementary capabilities of Instant and AutoThink. Stages 1 and
2 establish and specialize probabilistic decision prediction, while Stage 3 equips AutoThink with explicit reasoning
through supervision and reinforcement learning, preserving its single-pass prediction capability.
2.3.1 Stage 1: Full-Parameter SFT
Stage 1 establishes the foundational decision capabilities shared by all StartLux-Decision models through full-
parameter supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on Lux-Release, our initial training mixture.
Training Data.Lux-Release integrates diverse public datasets reformulated as typed decisions, spanning classifica-
tion, natural language inference, question answering, safety, preference modeling, and tool-use tasks. Additional
sources include community collections of typed decisions, benchmark training splits, multiple-choice questions
covering knowledge and commonsense reasoning, and a smaller portion of programmatically generated examples
(Appendix A). The training mixture contains approximately 2.7 million examples for models ranging from 0.8B
to 9B parameters, whereas the 27B model uses approximately 0.8 million examples, with a greater proportion of
benchmark training splits and multiple-choice questions. To mitigate answer-position bias, we deterministically per-
mute candidate options for each example using a hash-based procedure. To reduce training–evaluation overlap, we
exclude examples matching the Decision Index test sets or any of the 231 public JevBench v1.4.2 items (Benchmark
Heaven, 2026), together with all other examples belonging to the same parent groups (Appendix A).
Training Procedure.We fine-tune all language-model parameters, including the output projection, using soft
cross-entropy between the target and predicted probability distributions over candidate options at the final prompt
position. This objective directly optimizes the option-letter probability readout without introducing an additional

Stage 2 extends the shared decision foundation along two complementary dimensions: broader task coverage and
attention adaptation. We train adapters on Lux-Rounds, our second training mixture, and subsequently merge
them into the Stage 1 model weights. The Instant models adopt bidirectional attention in full-attention layers, while
the AutoThink models retain causal attention to support subsequent reasoning training.
Training Data.Lux-Rounds comprises approximately 1.7 million decision examples designed to broaden task
coverage beyond Stage 1. Most examples are drawn from benchmark training splits and related public datasets,
supplemented bybreadthdata covering decision types not represented in the Decision Index. Additional sources
include programmatically generated procedural decisions and select-all questions, API-generated examples, and
teacher-labeled decisions. We retain API-generated examples only when two independent verifier models agree
with their labels. A subset of Lux-Release is also retained to preserve the capabilities established in Stage 1. GSM8K
contributes only training problems formatted according to the revised DI 0.3 benchmark.
Training Procedure.We optimize the adapters using the same decision objective as Stage 1 before merging them
into the model weights. Each released Instant model is obtained by uniformly averaging the weights of three
independently trained runs at 0.8B or four runs at 2B–27B, differing only in their random seeds. Following weight
averaging, we refit the temperature parameters for probability calibration. The AutoThink models use the same
Stage 2 data but retain causal attention, preparing them for autoregressive reasoning in Stage 3.

2.3.3 Stage 3: Reasoning Supervision and RL
Stage 3 equips the causal Stage 2 models with explicit reasoning capabilities for decisions requiring multi-step
computation, while preserving efficient single-pass prediction. We first apply reasoning supervision to improve
reasoning generation and termination, followed by reinforcement learning to optimize reasoning for decision
quality and computational efficiency. The reasoning process and both probability readouts share model weights.
Reasoning Supervision.Decision-oriented training can substantially impair a model’s ability to generate and
reliably terminate reasoning traces. In our experiments, the decision-trained 4B model produces little or no reasoning
for approximately 40% of questions on which it is prompted to reason, while the 9B model continues generating
until the length limit on approximately 70% of such questions. These contrasting failure modes highlight the need
to train both reasoning generation and termination, with the latter also posing challenges for general reasoning
models (Chen et al., 2025). We first train an adapter on Lux-Think, using approximately 4,000–21,000 supervised
reasoning examples per model. Related work has explored supervised training, distillation, and reinforcement
learning to develop reasoning capabilities in smaller models (Zhuang et al., 2025). Following reasoning supervision,
all evaluated AutoThink models achieve higher post-reasoning accuracy than with single-pass prediction.
Despite these improvements, reasoning supervision does not fully resolve termination failures or excessive reasoning
length. We therefore introduce RLCD 2 to optimize the reasoning policy for probabilistic decision quality, rewarding
reasoning-induced improvements in decision probabilities while imposing a soft penalty on overlong reasoning
traces. This final training stage further improves reasoning efficiency while largely preserving decision accuracy, as
detailed in Section 3.

3. RLCD 2: Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Deliberative Decisions
Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD) was introduced alongside Jev, a hosted decision model
whose training procedure has not been publicly disclosed (Almeida, 2026). OpenJev-RLCD subsequently explores a
two-stage approach that calibrates post-rationale probability readouts before optimizing reasoning with proper
scoring rules (Gao and Wang, 2026). Building on this line of research, we propose RLCD 2, extending decision-
oriented reinforcement learning to explicitly optimize the contribution of reasoning to probabilistic decision quality.
Our formulation combines a frozen probability scorer, a decision-weighted proper scoring rule, and a soft length
penalty to balance decision quality with reasoning efficiency.

3.4 Reward Design and Reasoning Budget
Reasoning Budget and Accuracy Trade-offs.The soft reasoning budget determines when the length penalty
takes effect, governing the trade-off between reasoning efficiency and accuracy on computation-intensive decisions
(Table 3). Before RL, the 4B AutoThink model generates approximately 2,100 tokens per trace on DecisionPivot and
achieves 60.4% pair accuracy at the default confidence gate. A 512-token budget approximately halves reasoning
length but reduces pair accuracy to 52.9%. Increasing the budget to 2,048 and 3,072 tokens restores pair accuracy
to 59.2% and 59.9%, respectively, with the latter differing from the pre-RL checkpoint by −0.4 points (interval
[−1.8, +0.8]). Removing the length penalty increases pair accuracy to 61.7% but produces 40% longer traces,
with approximately one third failing to terminate at the 4,096-token generation limit, compared with about one
tenth under the 3,072-token budget. Across these configurations, held-out accuracy changes by at most 0.6 points,
while budgets of 512–3,072 tokens achieve approximately 99% termination. At the shorter budget, both Brier and
decision-weighted rewards improve calibration and termination but reduce DecisionPivot accuracy, highlighting
the cost of overly restrictive reasoning.

Termination Dynamics and Reward Components.The substantial reduction in reasoning length at 9B is associated
with repetitive generation loops that persist until the token limit. Early in RL training, 29% of 9B traces reach this
limit, compared with 9% at 4B. Penalizing overlong traces lowers their rewards and the group mean, increasing

negative relative advantages, although their share of the rollouts increases from 1% to 3% as termination improves.

components. Including the length penalty produces nearly universal termination even when the predictive
reward is replaced with random noise, whereas removing it results in longer traces and more termination failures.
Calibration improvements, however, depend on the predictive reward: proper-score rewards outperform accuracy-
only rewards, while replacing the length penalty with a termination bonus provides no calibration improvement.

rewards contribute to probability calibration.

We evaluate decision accuracy and probability reliability across tasks with varying computational demands. Decision
Index 0.2.1 and 0.3 assess performance on established benchmarks, while DecisionPivot examines computation-

4.1 Experimental Setup
All StartLux-Decision models are evaluated under fixed inference configurations, including the prompt format,
probability readout, and checkpoint-specific calibration temperatures described in Section 2. Neither model
parameters nor calibration temperatures are fitted on the evaluation sets (Appendix D). Decision Index (DI) 0.2.1
aggregates chance-corrected scores across 38 benchmarks spanning five domains. DI 0.3 removes ForecastBench and
replaces the original GSM8K questions with 2,638 reconstructed requests designed to eliminate answer-revealing
cues in the candidate options. The public evaluation accounts for 20% of the DI 0.3 leaderboard score, while private

5. Capability and Reliability Analysis
Beyond overall benchmark performance, we investigate how training choices and adaptive reasoning shape decision
performance, and assess the reliability of the resulting probability estimates. We further examine how downstream
selection and routing affect confidence reliability and how policy-aware calibration addresses these effects.
5.1 Effects of Training Data and Attention Design
To distinguish gains from task-family exposure and attention adaptation, we evaluate additional tasks beyond
Decision Index, whose public part also guided development choices (Section 4.1). Additional controlled training
runs use the 4B model; analyses of the released AutoThink models cover the 4B, 9B, and 27B scales.
Effects of Training Data.Additional decision-training data primarily improve performance on represented task
families. On the reused tasks of DecisionPivot, adding Stage 2 data from rating, safety, preference, and logic families
improves the 18 corresponding tasks by 8.6 points, compared with only 0.3 points on tasks outside those families.
Across the evaluated data groups, we find no clear improvement outside their respective task-family coverage. A
similar pattern emerges for procedural decisions: adding related training families raises the 4B model’s accuracy on
generated answerable-fact decisions from 61.5% to 75.8%. These results indicate task-family specialization, with
limited evidence of transfer to unrepresented families.

5.2 Adaptive Reasoning and Prediction Fusion
Benefits of Adaptive Reasoning.AutoThink exhibits its largest advantages over Instant on tasks requiring multi-
step computation (Figure 12). At 4B, the AutoThink model exceeds its Instant counterpart by 26 accuracy points
on reconstructed GSM8K problems, 22 on CRUXEval, 10 on GPQA Diamond, and 11 on MMLU-Pro. The pattern
persists at larger scales. At 9B, AutoThink exceeds Instant by 24.4 points on GSM8K and 14.4 on CRUXEval, with
a 0.5-point overall DI 0.3 advantage. At 27B, Instant already achieves 83% accuracy on reconstructed GSM8K
problems, with AutoThink scoring another 13.6 points higher. AutoThink also leads by 19 points on GPQA
Diamond, 10 on CRUXEval, and 9 on MMLU-Pro, while increasing DI 0.3 by 0.5 points. These comparisons involve
complete model variants; the following analysis isolates the effect of reasoning within fixed AutoThink checkpoints.
Confidence-Guided Reasoning.Within each AutoThink checkpoint, varying the confidence threshold from 0
(never reason) to 1 (always reason) reveals the accuracy–computation trade-off (Figure 13). Routing low-confidence
predictions to reasoning captures most of the attainable accuracy gains. On held-out examples from the training
distribution, the 9B model gains 1.0 accuracy point over its single-pass readout at the default threshold of 0.9,
averaging 596 reasoning tokens per example; always invoking reasoning yields no further gain. At 27B, the same
threshold yields a 2.5-point gain with 273 reasoning tokens per example.
The gains are more pronounced on DecisionPivot, where decisions often require explicit computation. At the default
threshold, the 9B model achieves 59.9% pair accuracy with 1,365 reasoning tokens per example, compared with
22.3% when reasoning is disabled in the same checkpoint. The corresponding 27B results are 86.9% pair accuracy
with 304 tokens per example, versus 48.0% without reasoning. RLCD 2 further improves the accuracy–efficiency
trade-off at smaller scales. At 9B, it raises DecisionPivot pair accuracy from 53.3% to 59.9% while reducing
reasoning-token consumption by 40%; at 4B, token consumption decreases by 25% with pair accuracy changing by
less than 0.5 points. The 27B model shows little change, consistent with its short reasoning traces (Section 3.4).

Combining Single-Pass and Post-Reasoning Predictions.For questions routed to reasoning, AutoThink combines
initial single-pass and post-reasoning probability distributions through a weighted average. The fusion weight is
particularly important for computation-intensive decisions, where initial predictions can be confidently incorrect.
On DecisionPivot, the 9B model achieves 56.6% pair accuracy with equal weights, 59.9% with a post-reasoning
weight of 75%, and 61.5% using only post-reasoning probabilities.
However, maximizing benchmark accuracy does not necessarily optimize probability quality. On held-out examples
from the training distribution, initial predictions are already accurate, whereas post-reasoning predictions exhibit
some overconfidence, consistent with prior observations (Lacombe et al., 2025). We therefore select the fusion weight
by minimizing the default prediction’s Brier score on held-out data. A post-reasoning weight of 75% minimizes this
score at 9B and 27B and is close to optimal at 4B, motivating the default 75%–25% combination.
Relative to equal weighting, the selected fusion improves DecisionPivot pair accuracy by 2.7 points at 4B but
reduces held-out accuracy by 0.5 points at 9B and 0.3 points at 4B. DI 0.3 scores change by less than 0.15 points.
At 27B, increasing the post-reasoning weight to 75% reduces held-out calibration error from 3.7 to 1.6 points,
with little change in accuracy. In contrast, on Decision Index, the same weight increases calibration error by
0.8–2.0 points relative to equal weighting, according to the leaderboard’s calibration metric. These results highlight
the importance of selecting fusion weights based on both probability quality and accuracy under the intended
evaluation distribution.
Computational Cost and Remaining Limitations.Adaptive reasoning incurs additional generation cost for routed
decisions. At the default threshold, the 4B, 9B, and 27B AutoThink models invoke reasoning on approximately
33%, 29%, and 28% of Decision Index questions, averaging 372, 271, and 110 reasoning tokens per question,
respectively. These averages include questions answered directly without reasoning. Instant remains preferable for
latency-sensitive applications, as it avoids autoregressive generation (Section 2.2).

5.3 Evidence Sensitivity and Probability Consistency
We examine whether decision probabilities respond to relevant evidence and remain consistent across related
question formulations. Both analyses use single-pass readouts, with reasoning disabled for AutoThink.
Evidence Sensitivity.We assess how predicted probabilities respond to removing supporting evidence. For HoVer
claims, we remove one supporting sentence; for HotpotQA questions reformulated as binary decisions, we remove
one gold supporting paragraph. Both probes use evaluation items only, and no evaluation data is used in training.
Across model scales, removing supporting evidence reduces the probability assigned to the supported answer in
90–97% of cases, with mean decreases ranging from 0.29 to 0.61. By contrast, adding an irrelevant sentence or
removing a distractor paragraph changes the corresponding probability by less than 0.015 on average (Figure 14a).
These results indicate sensitivity to supporting evidence with limited responses to irrelevant perturbations.
Probability Consistency.We assess whether predicted probabilities remain coherent across related question
formulations. For questions with mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive options, the affirmative probabilities
obtained by asking about each option separately as a yes-or-no decision should ideally sum to one. Following the
consistency test applied to Jev by Li et al. (2026), we measure the mean absolute deviation of this sum from one for
binary questions and extend the analysis to questions with more than two options. Consistency generally improves
with model scale: the mean deviation decreases from 0.25 for the 0.8B Instant model to 0.04 for the 27B Instant
model. AutoThink exhibits comparable deviations to Instant at 4B (0.11 versus 0.10) and 27B (0.04 for both), with a
lower deviation at 9B (0.11 versus 0.13).

Selection changes the conditional distribution of returned predictions. Choosing the highest-scoring candidate can
favor upward fluctuations in confidence estimates, an effect related to theoptimizer’s curse. Competing candidates’
scores also convey information about problem difficulty that the winning score alone may not capture. Consequently,
identical reported confidence can correspond to different empirical accuracies under different selection policies.
The same concern extends to confidence-dependent routing, including AutoThink’s think-gate and cascades that
escalate uncertain decisions to larger models (Gupta et al., 2024). Calibration and acceptance thresholds must
therefore be evaluated on the outputs of the deployed policy. Appendix C.2 formalizes these effects.
Selection and Routing Effects.We evaluate selection-induced miscalibration across generator–verifier combina-
tions. After conventional calibration on individual responses, selected responses are overconfident in 111 of 113
generator–verifier pairs at N= 16. The calibration gap increases with candidate count in all 126 evaluated runs.
Confidence-based routing exhibits a related effect: for 11 of 12 models, think-gate routing is associated with lower
accuracy at matched reported confidence. The direction of this shift depends on the task, as low initial confidence
can identify decisions that benefit substantially from additional computation on reasoning-intensive tasks.
Selection also affects confidence-based acceptance thresholds. We certify thresholds using a fixed-sequence Clopper–
Pearson test at 95% on the calibration half of each of 20 source-problem splits (Appendix C.3). Thresholds certified
on individual predictions need not preserve their error bounds after selection. For best-of-16 with a target error
rate of 5%, individually certified thresholds yield observed error rates above the target in 44 of 67 generator–verifier
pairs. By contrast, no such violations are observed when thresholds are certified on policy-selected responses. These
findings support policy-specific threshold certification.

7. Conclusion
StartLux-Decision combines efficient single-pass prediction with confidence-guided reasoning through a unified
probability interface. RLCD 2 improves reasoning termination and efficiency through proper-score rewards and
a soft length penalty, while largely preserving held-out decision accuracy. Evaluations on Decision Index and
DecisionPivot demonstrate strong decision performance, while policy-aware calibration mitigates reliability distor-
tions induced by selection and routing. Support for vLLM, llama.cpp, and MLX enables deployment across GPU
and local inference environments. Future work will focus on generalization beyond represented task families and
robust probability reliability under distribution shift. Together, these results advance efficient and reliable decision
modeling by integrating direct prediction, selective reasoning, and calibrated confidence.

Appendix D. Evaluation Details
This appendix details Decision Index scoring, per-benchmark results, evaluation safeguards, and inference for long
option lists and multiple questions. DecisionPivot is described by StartLux (2026); its data and scorer are available
on Hugging Face.
D.1 Decision Index Evaluation
Decision Index 0.2.1 aggregates 38 benchmarks across five weighted domains, with within-domain weights of 1.0
or 1.2. Individual scores are chance-corrected as (s−c)/( 1 −c) and clipped to [0, 1], where s is accuracy and c
is chance accuracy; unanswered requests count as incorrect. DI 0.3 retains five domains and includes 37 public
benchmarks. Table D.1 reports per-benchmark results for the released models and three reference systems. Repeated
evaluations with independently sampled reasoning traces vary by approximately 0.05 index points.
At 27B, AutoThink scores 11.8 points below Instant on MuSR, although invoking reasoning within AutoThink
changes accuracy by only +0.5 points. In a controlled Stage 2 comparison using identical data and the same Stage 1
checkpoint (Figure 4b), causal and bidirectional models score 50.3 and 64.9 on MuSR, respectively. Three additional
bidirectional seeds score 55.8–58.1, yielding gains of 5.5–14.6 points over the causal model and suggesting that
attention direction contributes to the cross-variant difference. On SATA-Bench, Stage 3 and explicit reasoning
account for 4.4 of the 6.7-point cross-variant difference.

D.2 GSM8K Benchmark Reconstruction
Some distractors in DI 0.2.1’s ten-choice GSM8K format provide cues to the correct answer, creating an answer-
selection shortcut. DI 0.3 replaces these distractors with answers from other problems to reduce this artifact. Earlier
checkpoints score 0.7–1.8 points higher on DI 0.2.1 with the original format, whereas the 4B Instant model trained
only on reconstructed questions shows no such advantage.
D.3 Evaluation Reliability and Serving Configuration
Valid evaluation runs require at least 99.9% request completion; unanswered requests count as incorrect. Prefix
caching and chunked prefill are disabled because they can alter decision readouts under the evaluated vLLM 0.19.1
configuration (Table D.2).
