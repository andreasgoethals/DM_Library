An ordinary-looking background detail can turn a correct model decision into a confident mistake. We demon-
strate this fragility in four decision systems, including Jev , across seven datasets covering knowledge, reason-
ing, and tool routing. Within 64 accepted target evaluations per decision, we uncover short context addi-
tions that redirect 61.4%–73.2% of each system’s initially correct decisions toward a wrong option fixed in
advance. The additions supply background or procedural information rather than explicit answer-selection
instructions, leaving the original question and choices intact. We construct them through probability-guided
context optimization, which uses shifts in the option distribution to refine surrounding text under naturalness
and answer-preservation constraints. Redirection affects initially confident decisions, often produces high-
confidence wrong choices, and transfers across models. In blinded human evaluation, 91.6% of 250 sampled
successful contexts are judged natural, answer-preserving, and free of decisive answer-changing evidence by a
majority of three independent annotators. These findings expose a weakness in current decision models: con-
text that looks entirely compatible with an input can redirect the choices that agents, routers, and evaluators
rely on.

1. Introduction
Language-model systems increasingly turn open-ended language into bounded choices that drive
downstream behavior . An agent selects a tool or its next action, a router directs a request, and a
model-based evaluator chooses among candidate responses ( Schick et al. , 2023; Yao et al. , 2023;
Zheng et al. , 2023; Patil et al. , 2025). We study decision models that expose this step as a prob-
ability distribution over a specified set of options. This interface allows downstream software to
use model probabilities directly , without first interpreting a free-form response. These decisions are
made in context: inputs can include conversation history , background details, retrieved evidence, or
descriptions of the surrounding state.
A detail can fit naturally with an input without changing which option is correct. We find that
short additions of this kind can nevertheless redirect an initially correct decision toward a wrong
option fixed in advance. We call this phenomenonnatural-context redirection. Rather than instructing
the model to select an answer , the addition supplies background or procedural detail relevant to the
original input. The question and available choices stay unchanged, yet the selected option and its
probability can shift sharply . Figure 1 illustrates this behavior on a climate-ethics question: an added
sentence about intergenerational consequences redirects the decision without changing the definition
being asked for .
Prior work shows that language models are sensitive to prompt formatting, demonstration or-
der , option order , and the placement of relevant evidence ( Ishibashi et al. , 2023; Lu et al. , 2022;
Pezeshkpour and Hruschka , 2024; Liu et al. , 2024). Fluent, answer-preserving additions can also
mislead question-answering models, while model-based evaluators exhibit systematic sensitivity to

carries over to finite-choice decision interfaces, where both the selected option and its probability
can affect downstream behavior . We study whether naturally fitting additions can redirect a correct
choice toward a fixed wrong option, and use the full option distribution to measure and uncover that
redirection.
For each target model, we retain the items it initially answers correctly and fix one incorrect
option per item as the target. We construct answer-preserving context additions: the original source,
question, and choices stay fixed, and additions must fit naturally while preserving the correct answer .
Probability-guided context optimization uses the full option distribution to guide this construction.
Even before the selected option changes, a candidate can increase the target’s probability relative
to its strongest competitor . We use this graded signal to prioritize candidates for further refinement
under the same semantic constraints. A targeted flip occurs when the model selects the fixed wrong
target. Targeted Flip Rate (TFR) measures the proportion of initially correct decisions for which we
uncover at least one such flip within a fixed budget of accepted target evaluations.
We evaluate four decision systems—Jev , OpenSourceJev , Von, and a plain Qwen scorer—across
seven datasets covering knowledge, reasoning, and tool routing. Within 64 accepted target evalua-
tions per decision, context optimization uncovers targeted flips in 61.4%–73.2% of each system’s ini-
tially correct decisions. Redirection occurs in every model–dataset combination, including next-tool
choices in recorded dialogues. The effect reaches originally confident decisions, and many success-
ful contexts contain only a single added sentence. In a blinded evaluation with three independent
annotators, 91.6% of 250 successful contexts sampled across systems meet all three validity criteria
by majority judgment: naturalness, answer preservation, and absence of decisive answer-changing
evidence.

2. Related Work
Behavioral testing evaluates models against expectations about how their predictions should respond
to controlled input changes. CheckList includes invariance tests for changes that should leave a
prediction unchanged ( Ribeiro et al. , 2020). Adversarial reading comprehension gives a concrete
instance of this concern: Jia and Liang (2017) add distracting sentences to passages without chang-
ing their correct answers, yet substantially degrade model performance. T ASA combines edits to
the answer-bearing sentence with a fluent distracting sentence while preserving the gold answer
(Cao et al. , 2022). These studies show that preserving the correct answer does not ensure a stable
model prediction. We examine the same tension in finite-choice decision models, keeping the origi-
nal source and option schema intact while adding naturally fitting context. We measure redirection
from a correct decision to one wrong option fixed before construction, along with the probability
assigned to that option.
Model-based evaluation studies a related reliability problem when model judgments determine
rankings, preferences, or scores ( Zheng et al. , 2023). Controlled studies find sensitivity to response
position and other features beyond the quality being assessed ( Ye et al. , 2025; Shi et al. , 2025).
Raina et al. (2024) optimize universal phrases that alter assessments and transfer across evaluators,
with stronger effects on absolute scoring than on comparative assessment. Our contexts are instead
constructed for individual decision items and must preserve their correct answers. We focus on
redirecting a choice with a known correct answer , rather than changing how a candidate response
is assessed. The construction process also connects to automatic prompt optimization, which uses
evaluated proposals or textual feedback to refine language inputs ( Pryzant et al. , 2023; Yang et al. ,
2024). Here, the refinement signal is the target’s probability relative to its strongest competitor ,
and the editable text is the surrounding context rather than the task instruction. Appendix A covers
learned context generation and concurrent decision-model studies.
3. Natural-Context Redirection
3.1. Setting and Context Additions
We study language-based decision models that return probabilities over a finite set of options. A
decision u = ( x, q, C, y) consists of source context x, question q, options C, and correct option y.
The model assigns probabilities pθ(c | x, q, C) and returns a selected option byθ. We retain the pop-
ulation Uθ whose original selection is correct and fix the most probable wrong option as the target:
tu = arg maxc∈C\{ y} pθ(c | x, q, C). The target remains fixed throughout construction. Multi-answer
tasks use binary option-membership decisions, retaining one eligible absent option per source item
(Appendix B.2).

without changing its correct answer . A context z = ((b1, a1), . . . ,(bL, aL)) specifies added sentences

A language-model proposer , conditioned on the original item, gold answer , fixed target, and cur-

Each iteration extends sampled parents and archives their evaluated children for the next itera-
tion. Original-input restarts explore new additions alongside refinements of previous contexts. We
use 16 proposal slots for up to four iterations, reserving four slots for original-input restarts after
the first iteration. Each rejected proposal gets one retry and uses no target call. Construction stops
after a completed iteration reaches a targeted flip with probability at least 0.7, or at the iteration
limit, giving at most 64 accepted target evaluations. Appendix D provides pseudocode, the allocation
derivation, and complete execution settings.
3.3. Learning to Generate Contexts
Optimization trajectories also provide supervision for proposing contexts on new items. Each tra-
jectory records an original decision, its fixed target, the generated additions, and their resulting
margins. These records support two forms of supervision: predicting a useful next addition from an
existing context, or predicting a complete successful context from the original item.
We adapt the same base proposer with LoRA ( Hu et al. , 2021) while fixing the target model and
checker . V1 learns next-addition transitions, weighting examples within each item by their result-
ing margin. V2 learns complete successful contexts with item-balanced supervision before length
filtering. Both use the same backbone, adapter configuration, and optimizer . At evaluation, a com-
mon complete-context prompt tests direct generation on held-out items without intermediate target
feedback. Appendix H gives the loss, corpus construction, length filtering, and training schedules.

4.4. Probability Feedback and Evaluation Budget
Redirections appear early in optimization, and additional evaluations continue to uncover new cases.
Across all four systems in Figure 4a, most of the final successes are already found within the first 16
accepted calls. The curves continue upward through the full budget, with diminishing gains.
Target information already helps at the first proposal: target-aware one-shot outperforms neutral
generation on every system in Table 1. Repeated independent generation uncovers many more flips,
reaching 48.0% TFR on the same 508 Jev decisions under the optimizer’s 64-call ceiling. Full context
optimization adds a further 13.4 percentage points. The two procedures find nearly the same number
of flips at the first 16-call prefix; their coverage separates as more evaluations become available.
Independent proposals discover much of the phenomenon, while the complete refinement procedure
extends coverage beyond this control. Appendix J reports the paired outcomes and budget curves.
Probability feedback helps decide which contexts merit further refinement. We compare
probability-only and label-only allocation on 140 matched initially correct Jev decisions, with
numerical history hidden from the proposer in both conditions. The former weights parents by
target margin; the latter uses a binary indicator of target selection. Until a flip occurs, that binary
score gives the same value to every archived context, whereas the margin distinguishes candidates
closer to selecting the target.
At 64 accepted evaluations, probability-based allocation improves TFR by 7.1 percentage points
over label-based allocation. The paired differences in Figure 4b increase with the evaluation budget;
at 64 calls, the 95% interval excludes zero. Here the probabilities help through parent selection: the
proposer receives no numerical history in either condition. Full feedback, which also exposes that
history to the proposer , does not exceed the observed probability-only rate. The full paired analysis
is in Appendix G.1.

vide complementary ways to uncover redirections: the trained proposers improve the first genera-
tion, and selection among several generations adds coverage for each. Appendix H gives the training
recipes, task-level results, and paired tests.
5. Conclusion
Natural-context redirection exposes a weakness in how decision models use surrounding information.
Across four systems and seven datasets, short additions can redirect an initially correct decision to a
fixed wrong option while preserving the underlying task and answer . This behavior reaches originally
confident choices and can persist when a context is transferred to another model. Probability-guided
context optimization makes these failures observable by refining additions that shift the option dis-
tribution.
Bounded decisions connect language to software actions. Our results show that their reported
probabilities can shift with details that leave the appropriate choice unchanged. Evaluating these
interfaces therefore requires examining how surrounding context affects both the selected option
and its probability . A dependable decision model must distinguish context that changes the correct
choice from context that merely changes which option it favors.
Limitations
The evaluation covers English tasks with finite option sets and known correct answers. These tasks
make it possible to distinguish contextual redirection from a legitimate change in the answer . Extend-
ing this analysis to multilingual inputs, open-ended actions, or decisions without an agreed reference
answer requires corresponding criteria for answer preservation.
We evaluate individual decisions, including next-tool choices within recorded dialogues. In an
interactive system, a redirected action can change subsequent observations and decisions. Measuring
how redirection propagates through such sequences requires evaluation in a closed-loop environment.
The present decision-level results identify a source of fragility at the interface through which those
systems select their actions.

Ethical Considerations
The evaluation uses public benchmark tasks and bounded choices to study decision reliability . Con-
texts that redirect routing or tool selection have dual-use implications when transferred to oper-
ational systems. The intended use is to identify and measure these failure modes before models
control downstream actions. Testing operational systems should be conducted with authorization
and safeguards against unintended actions.

A. Expanded Related Work
Concurrent work and historical background. The recent studies of Jev and typed decision models
discussed below appeared concurrently with this work, in September and early October 2026. We
discuss them as parallel investigations, separately from the earlier literature on prompt sensitivity ,
adversarial text, and model-based evaluation.
Decision interfaces and their implementations. Concurrent work develops several ways to ex-
pose a finite option distribution from a language model. AnyJev restricts a frozen instruction-tuned
model’s next-token distribution to option labels, then corrects label priors and averages across cyclic
option rotations ( Zhang et al. , 2026b). InnerJev instead distills a model’s reasoning-end answer
distribution into a first-token decision readout ( Duan et al. , 2026). Visual Jev extends the inter-
face to visual inputs, sharing image and public-context computation across isolated decision queries
(Yu and Yao, 2026). These approaches separate the decision interface from any single model archi-
tecture: finite-choice probabilities can come from a trained decision model, a distilled readout, or a
constrained language-model scorer . Our study examines context redirection across implementations,
including interfaces that share the same underlying weights.
Concurrent evaluations and applications. JEVal evaluates decision systems across domains,
languages, and prompting configurations ( Duan et al. , 2026). Its results distinguish strong perfor-
mance on evidence-explicit inputs from weaknesses involving specialized knowledge, calibration,
and accumulated errors in longer agent interactions. The evidence audit by Tang and Zheng (2026)
likewise separates the validity of a typed readout from correctness, calibration, and downstream
utility . These distinctions are useful for interpreting structured outputs: a well-formed distribution
specifies what software can consume, while its reliability depends on the decision and the context
that produced it.
Concurrent workflow studies make the consequences of those choices more concrete. Ling et al.
(2026) document uses of Jev in public repositories, including routing, scoring, tool selection, and ac-
tion selection. REFLEX places Jev in a selective-control cascade, using confidence to choose between
a fast decision and a stronger language-model fallback ( Wu and Lim , 2026b). Deng et al. (2026)
study semantic choices that select inputs or rules for scientific calculations, evaluating the selected
option, the resulting quantity , and the final claim separately . These settings show why a probability
shift can matter beyond the returned label: it may determine which computation runs or whether
further reasoning is invoked. Our fixed-option evaluation tests one prerequisite for such use, namely
whether an answer-preserving change in surrounding context redirects the selected decision.
Context-dependent reasoning and action. The need to use context selectively extends beyond
dedicated decision interfaces. SocialMaze evaluates reasoning over evolving social interactions and
uncertain information (Xu et al. , 2026c); in these tasks, models must distinguish what a participant
says from what the evidence supports. In concurrent work, GT A shows that graph-reasoning accuracy
depends on how the same graph is represented and uses a learned selector to choose representations
(Xu et al., 2026d). AlgoWorlds separates information gathering from the quality of a final structured
decision, using algorithmic environments with verifiable optima (Xu et al., 2026b). Formal-reasoning
evaluations similarly examine the soundness of generated program invariants rather than treating a
plausible reasoning trace as a correct result ( Mitchell et al. , 2025). These evaluations make differ-
ent aspects of context use observable. Here, the controlled question is whether additional context
changes the model’s choice when it should not change the answer .
Agent architectures make this dependence on context explicit. DyFlow updates reasoning work-
flows from intermediate results ( Wang et al., 2025b), while ReMindRAG uses language-model deci-
sions to guide graph traversal and replay useful traversal experience ( Hu et al. , 2025). QUITE con-

tary model subsets to improve answer coverage ( Wei et al. , 2026). Selecting capable components
and preserving the intended decision under contextual variation address different parts of an agent’s
reliability .
Behavioral expectations and prompt sensitivity . Behavioral testing specifies how predictions
should respond to controlled changes rather than treating all input variation alike. CheckList dis-
tinguishes minimum-functionality tests, invariance tests, and directional expectations ( Ribeiro et al.,
2020). For context additions, the relevant expectation depends on whether the new material changes
which answer is correct. Prompt-sensitivity studies examine other degrees of freedom: demonstra-
tion order ( Lu et al. , 2022), discrete templates ( Ishibashi et al. , 2023), option order ( Pezeshkpour
and Hruschka , 2024), and the position of relevant evidence ( Liu et al. , 2024). These interventions
show that presentation affects how models use otherwise available information. Our construction
preserves the existing text and choice schema, while introducing additional background or procedu-
ral detail. Its semantic requirement concerns the correct answer , not complete equivalence between
the original and augmented texts: an addition may introduce a relevant fact without changing the
answer .
Cross-Lingual Pitfalls uses automated bilingual question construction to identify cases a model
answers correctly in English but incorrectly in another language ( Xu et al. , 2025b). This examines
consistency across linguistic realizations of a question. Context redirection tests a different variation:
the original question remains intact, and only its surrounding text grows. Both approaches use
controlled input construction to expose failures that aggregate task scores can obscure.
Answer-preserving adversarial text. Jia and Liang (2017) show that adding a distracting sen-
tence can mislead reading-comprehension systems even when the passage still supports the same
answer . This is a direct precedent for testing a model through additions rather than rewriting the
original passage. T ASA further separates two operations: reducing support for the gold answer in the
model’s prediction by perturbing its sentence, and drawing the model toward a distractor through an
additional sentence ( Cao et al. , 2022). Its score-based construction also illustrates the use of model
feedback to select textual candidates. Adaptive Distraction extends this line to behavior-guided tree
search for semantically coherent, task-irrelevant additions that preserve the original question and
answer ( Wang et al. , 2025a). It directly connects adaptive text construction with contextual ro-
bustness in general-purpose language models. Our evaluation retains all original text and existing
options, fixes a wrong target before construction, and optimizes additions subject to naturalness
and answer-preservation constraints. This connects the QA intervention to decisions with an explicit
option distribution, across question, passage, and dialogue inputs.
Model-based evaluation. The LLM-as-a-judge literature examines whether model judgments
provide useful proxies for human preferences and how those judgments depend on their inputs
(Zheng et al. , 2023). Controlled transformations reveal biases associated with response position,
length, and other features ( Ye et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025). Raina et al. (2024) study both absolute
scoring and comparative assessment, optimizing a shared phrase on a surrogate assessor and testing
transfer to other models. Their absolute-scoring setting is substantially more susceptible than their
comparative setting. This is useful precedent for optimized text and transfer , but the intervention

Representation-level studies provide another view of why judgments change. BiasLens measures

Retrieval and ranking manipulation. Natural-looking text can influence which information a
system selects as well as how it evaluates that information. StealthRank optimizes text embedded

Multimodal Generative Engine Optimization combines textual suffixes with image perturbations to

fixed-choice problem with a known correct answer and measures whether an addition makes the
model select a specified wrong option.
Feedback-guided text optimization. Model feedback has been used to construct both effective

fixed wrong option and its strongest competitor . This margin allocates refinement among accepted
contexts, while separate checks implement the semantic constraints. The task instruction and option

same construction procedure.

Instruction override and jailbreaks. Prompt-injection evaluation tests whether models follow

Sensitivity to surface cues also affects models trained to resist injection. Li et al. (2026b) show
that defense-trained models can reject benign requests because of their position, trigger tokens, or

The concurrent Decision Hijacking study targets Jev’s typed decisions through malicious tool or

Counterfactual generation and explanation. Polyjuice generates controlled textual perturba-

reveal which changes accompany a different prediction. For natural-context redirection, a changed
prediction alone is insufficient: the context must still support the original correct answer , and the
new selection must be the preselected wrong option. This distinguishes a redirection witness from
an edit that legitimately changes the answer . Our representative-context rule favors fewer additions
after the targeted outcome and confidence criteria are met. It provides compact examples of the
observed failure, rather than minimum-edit explanations of the model’s decision.

C.1. Independent Human Validation
Sampling and blinding. We randomly sampled 250 successful optimization contexts, stratified
across datasets, with balanced sampling across the four target systems and deduplication. We mixed
them with 100 contexts that passed the automatic acceptance checks but did not produce a targeted
flip. Three independent annotators evaluated these augmented items without seeing the designated
target, the model’s prediction, or whether the context caused a flip. Mixing the two groups allows
the same semantic criteria to be applied without revealing the model outcome. Annotators assess
each addition within the complete augmented item.
Judgment criteria. Annotators judged three properties: whether the addition fits naturally with
the original input; whether the original gold answer remains uniquely correct; and whether the
addition introduces decisive evidence that materially changes the answer . Related background or
a change in emphasis is not, by itself, answer-changing evidence. The decisive-evidence criterion
concerns whether the added information changes which option is correct, rather than whether it
influences the model. A context is human-valid only when a majority of annotators judge it natural,
gold-preserving, and free of decisive answer-changing evidence.

available as parents in the following iteration. The complete procedure is given in Algorithm 1.
Frozen configuration. The primary procedure uses K = 16 , R = 4 , T = 1 , η = 0 .1, ρ = 0 .25,
and τs = 0 .7. All first-iteration proposals start from the original item. Later iterations have four
scheduled original-input restarts and twelve archive allocations. Since each child adds one sentence
to a context from an earlier iteration, the construction has at most four additions. The total number
of accepted target evaluations is at most KR = 64 , excluding the original evaluation used to select
eligible items.
Rejections, retries, and stopping. Each slot allows at most two generation attempts. A failed
mechanical check, semantic rejection, or duplicate rendered state leads to a retry when one remains;
an exhausted slot makes no target call. Every accepted context is evaluated once, whether or not
it changes the selected option. Stopping is checked after the entire iteration, so an early successful
candidate does not cancel the remaining slots in that iteration. Runs may use fewer than 64 target
calls because of early stopping or rejected slots. Budget curves use accepted-call prefixes, which need
not coincide with iteration ends.
Representative context. For a successful item, selection first prefers contexts with target proba-
bility at least 0.7, then fewer additions, higher target probability , and earlier target-call index. This
rule selects a compact observed context without deleting any additions or re-evaluating edited ver-
sions. Source items without a targeted flip use their highest-margin evaluated context for transfer ,

Systematic resampling. For M archive-allocated slots, draw a seeded offset ξ uniformly from

slot, and retry index. Archive resampling has its own seed derived from the item and iteration.
D.3. Feedback Conditions
The primary condition uses margins for parent allocation and supplies the proposer with the original
and current target probabilities, original and current margins, and their difference. These fields de-
scribe the selected parent’s progress and remain outside the generated context. The checker receives
none of them.
The matched feedback comparison changes the score used for allocation. Probability-only uses
mu(zj), whereas label-only substitutes 1[byθ(xzj , q, C) = tu]. Both omit numeric history from the
proposer input. Before any targeted flip, label-only therefore allocates uniformly over archive en-
tries, in addition to scheduled restarts. Proposal settings, acceptance checks, budgets, and the 0.7
probability-threshold stopping rule are shared.

D.4. Experimental Protocol
Primary context optimization. Each target first evaluates all 700 held-out source items. Context
optimization then runs on its own initially correct population shown in Table 5. The fixed target
option is selected from the clean distribution before any proposal call. The primary condition uses
16 slots for up to four complete iterations, a 0.7 early-stop threshold, and the automatic acceptance
pipeline in Appendix C.
One-shot controls. Neutral and target-aware one-shot conditions operate on the same eligible
decisions as the primary condition. Each gets exactly one proposal opportunity from the clean root.
Rates keep the full initially correct denominator , with zero outcomes for rejected or unsuccessful pro-
posals. Comparing neutral and target-aware generation isolates access to the fixed alternative. Ap-
pendix J extends target-aware generation to repeated independent proposals under the optimizer’s
accepted-call ceiling.
Probability-feedback comparison. The matched Jev subset contains 20 clean-correct decisions
from each of the seven datasets, chosen by the lowest seeded SHA-256 ranks. Full feedback uses
probabilities for archive allocation and supplies numerical history to the proposer . Probability-only
keeps probability-guided allocation while hiding that history . Label-only replaces archive margins
with a binary indicator of whether the fixed target is selected. All other prompts, proposer settings,
acceptance rules, restarts, seeds, and call budgets remain fixed.
Transfer protocol. For every ordered source–destination pair , the representative source context
is frozen and rendered unchanged for the destination. Matching occurs on the exact source-selected
unit, not merely the source item identifier . The destination must have selected its own gold branch
on that clean unit. The source-fixed wrong branch is mapped through the shared choice schema and
defines transfer success.
Learned context generation. Both training recipes use development records and are evaluated
on the same 508 initially correct Jev decisions. The historical Base–V1 comparison generates one
addition per proposal; the current Base–V1–V2 comparison generates a complete list of one to four
additions. Sample zero defines one-generation performance, and best-of-four selects the accepted
context with highest target margin. Appendix H.4 gives the shared settings and the differences
between these protocols.

I.4. Construction Outcomes
Accepted but ineffective context. Many coherent additions preserve the correct answer yet leave
the gold branch selected or move probability toward a wrong branch other than tu. These states
still enter the archive, consume one accepted target evaluation, and can become parents in later
iterations. They contribute to optimization through their margin but count as failures in TFR until
the fixed target is selected.
Generation outcomes. Illegal boundaries, empty text, normalized duplicate additions, explicit
selection phrases, and malformed structured outputs are rejected before a target call. A duplicate
rendered state is also removed by its content hash. Each slot receives one retry , after which the next
scheduled slot proceeds. These outcomes affect proposal usage but never change the decision-level
denominator .
