What Can’t Jev Decide? Probing the Limits of One-Pass Decision Models
Wang Yang1, Xinpeng Li1, Yanyan Zhang1, Chuang Ma2, Shouren Wang1, Jianzhe Wei3,
Liqin Ye3,Xiaoran Cai 4,Mohsen Hariri 1,Vipin Chaudhary 1,Xiaotian Han 1
1Case Western Reserve University,2NII LLMC (Japan),

Abstract
Jev and its open replicas choose among the
options a caller supplies with a single forward
pass, fast enough to decide inside an agent loop.
We ask what such a model cannot decide, and
find a simple limit: Jev cannot make a decision
that an LLM makes only by writing out inter-
mediate steps. On a benchmark that varies one
factor at a time, Jev finds and selects records
without error up to 20k tokens and 64 options,
but once the answer depends on two or more
updates of one user, accuracy falls, to 53–80%
at sixteen updates, because similar records in-
terfere. Given a stale summary of the state, Jev
follows it. On real decision items graded by
how many thinking tokens a reference LLM
needs, Jev answers 97.5% of the items that one
forward pass of that LLM solves, but only 74%
of the others. Jev also does not know its limit:
its confidence flags the errors it makes itself
but not those it inherits from a stale input, and
neither an explicit nor a trained hand-off option
catches the latter. The open models we evalu-
ate share these limits. Checking the state such
models rely on requires a model that reasons.
1 Introduction

3 Where Jev Breaks: A Controlled
Diagnosis
3.1 A benchmark that varies one factor at a
time
Design.Each item consists of a context, a ques-
tion and 2–64 options, and its answer is computed
by a program rather than labelled by a model (Fig-
ure 2). Contexts range from 1k to 20k tokens.
For each scenario, shorter contexts are prefixes
of longer ones and the target records keep their rel-
ative position, so neighbouring cells differ only in
the factor under test. Every wrong option also ap-
pears in the context, so no option can be ruled out
without reading it. Each cell contains 10 scenarios,
each with 3 option orders.
Tasks.Retrievalasks for the access code of one
user, hidden among other users’ records or among

3.2 Length and the number of options are not
the bottleneck
We first test whether finding and selecting a record
degrade with length, depth or the number of op-
tions. They do not: Jev answers all 105 retrieval,
selection and event-lookup cells correctly, up to
20k tokens and 64 options, with the target at any
depth and in either haystack, and puts a probability
close to 1 on the correct option. Long contexts,
buried targets and many options, the usual explana-
tions for failures of long-context models (Kamradt,
2023; Liu et al., 2024; Hsieh et al., 2024), therefore
do not explain the failures below.
3.3 State tracking fails from two updates on
We next ask for the user’s current state, which de-
pends on all k updates in the same log. Accuracy
drops as soon as k= 2 and falls to 53–80% at
k= 16 (Figure 3b), although Jev answers every
event lookup on these logs correctly. It finds each
update when asked about it, but does not use all of
them when the answer depends on all of them.
The errors show which updates are lost: the
later ones. Of 145 wrong answers, 134 are an
earlier state of the same user and 137 contain fewer
projects than the correct set; only 3 confuse users.
The probability on the correct option falls before
the accuracy does: it averages 0.93 in the multi-
update cells that are still answered perfectly, and
about 0.5 atk= 16.

We then change the log, keeping the question
and options, to find which of its properties causes
the failure (rows R1–R4). With only the target
user’s entries left (R1), Jev answers every item
correctly, so it can combine 16 updates. Moving
these entries, in order, into one block at the end of
the log while keeping every other entry (R3) leaves
the length and the other users’ records unchanged,
and accuracy at k= 16 stays at 64%. Replacing
the other users’ entries of R3 by essay text of the
same length (R4) raises it to 99%. The failure is
caused neither by scattered entries nor by length,
but by the similar records around them, a sharper
form of the distraction by irrelevant context known
from LLMs (Shi et al., 2023). Position does not
explain it either: listing the log newest first (R2)
lowers accuracy atk≥2from 80% to 72%.
This interference differs from the one reported
for LLMs, where earlier values of the same key
displace the latest one (Wang and Sun, 2025): R1
contains all of the target’s earlier updates and is
answered perfectly. What Jev fails at is keeping one
user’s updates apart from other users’ records. Kev-
27B behaves alike, except that R3 already suffices;
Kev-4B reaches only 67% at k= 16 even in R1
and R4, so at this scale the combination itself is
limited as well.
In practice the target’s history cannot be pulled
out of a real log by hand; the usual way to spare the
model the search is to state the result in advance,
which we test next.

3.5 A state summary is used, trusted, and not
checked
We next test the obvious remedy: compute the state
in advance and append it to the log as a one-line
summary. Jev uses the summary, but also trusts
it. With acorrectsummary, it answers the final-
state question correctly in all 30 cells. With astale
summary, which omits the last update, Jev picks
the stale state in 61–100% of the items that offer
it as an option, even though the log contradicts it,
and more often in longer logs (68% at 1k, 93% at
20k; Figure 3c).
Asked directly, Jev cannot tell a correct summary
from a stale one. It accepts 91% of correct sum-
maries but rejects only 62% of stale ones over all
context lengths (92% and 70% up to 8k, Table 2),
and almost none (0–7%) when the summary omits
one of two updates in a 16k–20k log. The remedy
is therefore only as good as the summary.
3.6 The failures are shared by the model class
We finally ask whether these failures are specific to
Jev. They are not: on contexts up to 8k tokens, ev-
ery open model in Table 2 shows the same pattern:
retrieval and selection are close to perfect, final
state drops once updates must be combined, and
a stale summary is followed. No decision model
verifies summaries reliably: averaged over correct
and stale summaries, none exceeds 85% (chance
50%), and the models that reject the most stale sum-
maries reject most correct ones as well (Qwen3.5-
4B-Base rejects 98% of stale and accepts 2% of
correct ones). Neither scale (0.8B to 35B), train-
ing on questions written by a reasoning teacher
(JevK5), nor reading the logits of an untuned LLM
changes this pattern. Two models in Appendix B
fail earlier, at retrieval itself: Kev-0.8B and the
contrastive CLM-8B.
Jev thus decides what one record says, but not
what several records imply together once similar
records surround them, nor whether a state it is
given is correct. Both are decisions that an LLM
makes by writing intermediate steps: with thinking,
Qwen3.8-27B answers 96% of the multi-update
items and rejects 95% of the stale summaries (Ta-
ble 2). Section 4 asks whether the same limit ap-
pears on real decision tasks.
4 Where Jev Breaks on Real Tasks
Real tasks offer no factor to vary, so we measure
how much reasoning each item demands and relate
Jev’s accuracy to it.

4.4 A reasoning backbone does not help
We finally test whether a backbone trained to rea-
son closes the gap. It does not. We train Kev
with its released recipe twice, once from Qwen3.5-
Base, as released, and once from the post-trained
Qwen3.5 that thinks before answering, three seeds
each (Table 9 in Appendix B). On the real-task
items the two backbones are indistinguishable:
Kev-4B reaches 87.2% and 87.4%, Kev-9B 90.1%
and 90.1%, and on the items that need reasoning
the difference is comparable to the spread across
seeds. On the final-state items of Section 3 up to
4k tokens, the reasoning backbone is worse: 70%
vs. 50% at 4B and 76% vs. 71% at 9B for k≥2 .
Whatever reasoning the backbone has learned is
exercised when it generates tokens; a model that
answers in one forward pass does not draw on it.
On real tasks as on synthetic logs, then, Jev loses
exactly the decisions that an LLM makes by writing
intermediate steps; a backbone trained to reason
does not recover them. Section 5 asks whether Jev
can tell when it meets such a decision.
5 Does It Know, and Can It Hand Off?
A system built on Jev must send the items Jev
would get wrong to a stronger model, either by
reading Jev’s confidencepmax or by offering it an
option to hand the question off.
5.1 Confidence flags Jev’s own errors, not
inherited ones
We first ask whether pmax separates Jev’s wrong
answers from its right ones. It does when Jev makes
the error itself: AUROC is 0.83 on the final-state
items, with an expected calibration error of 0.03,
and 0.94 on the real tasks. When the error comes
from a stale summary, however, the signal is gone:
AUROC is 0.53, and at k= 2 it is 0.26, so Jev is
more confident when it follows the stale summary
than when it does not. Every model shows the same
split (Figure 6a): on the synthetic logs its AUROC
is 0.68–0.93 for its own errors and 0.18–0.66 after
a stale summary, below chance for 7 of 11 models.
5.2 Jev does not hand off
We next offer an explicit way out, and Jev almost
never takes it. We add one option, “Cannot be de-
termined reliably from the state; hand this question
to a stronger model”, and one sentence asking the
model to choose it when unsure. Jev chooses it for
1.6% of the real-task items and 3 of its 40 errors,
none of them among its 29 errors on JevBench-
hard; on the synthetic logs it hands off 3 of 145
wrong answers. The open models hand off more,
but for every model except Open-Jev-27B a confi-
dence threshold that routes the same share of items
leaves a more accurate remainder (Table 4, top).
The models carry the signal but do not act on it.
5.3 Hand-off training helps where a threshold
cannot be tuned
We then ask whether the option can be trained in
without new data, keeping the training data and
number of steps of the original recipes. The train-
ing set is split in two halves; a model trained on
one half answers the other, and every question it
answers wrongly is marked as unlearnable. In the
final run each question carries the hand-off option
with probability 0.5, worded with paraphrases dis-
joint from the evaluation wording, and its target
is the hand-off option if the question is unlearn-
able and the answer otherwise. We train Kev-4B,
Kev-9B and JevK5-4B, three seeds each.
On the task the threshold was tuned for, training
makes the option useful but not better than the
threshold (Table 4, bottom). Accuracy without
the option is unchanged, and the share of errors
handed off doubles, from 23% to 47% for Kev-4B
and from 22% to 44% for Kev-9B. A threshold on

6 Related Work
Decision models.Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026) intro-
duced the interface we study; its replicas fine-tune
an LLM, wrap one, or read its option logits (Sec-
tion 2). Reading option scores from an LLM is also
how multiple-choice benchmarks are commonly
evaluated, and such scores depend on the prompt,
the option labels and their order (Zhao et al., 2021;
Zheng et al., 2024). Public evaluations of decision
models report accuracy and calibration over mixed
tasks (Benchmark Heaven, 2026; Jevals, 2026); we
instead isolate the inputs on which they fail.
Long contexts and state tracking.Retrieval
from long contexts degrades with length, the posi-
tion of the target and the difficulty of the task (Kam-
radt, 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Hsieh et al., 2024), and
reasoning over facts spread through them degrades
further (Kuratov et al., 2024). Language models
track entity states only partly (Kim and Schuster,
2023), and recalling the latest value of a key suffers
from interference by its earlier values (Wang and
Sun, 2025). Decision models retrieve without error
up to their limit, and in our logs the target’s own
earlier updates are harmless (R1); the failure comes
from similar records of other users.
What one forward pass can compute.Writing
intermediate steps improves LLM reasoning (Wei
et al., 2022), and theory shows why: bounded-
depth transformers provably cannot solve some
problems without them (Feng et al., 2023; Mer-
rill and Sabharwal, 2024), state tracking among
them (Merrill et al., 2024); LLMs also com-
plete multi-hop inferences only partly within one
pass (Yang et al., 2024). Decision models give
these steps up by design; we measure what this
costs on practical decisions, on synthetic logs as
well as on real tasks.
Knowing when to abstain.Large LLMs are well
calibrated on multiple-choice questions in a suit-
able format (Kadavath et al., 2022), and a threshold
on the maximum probability is the standard way
to abstain (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017). Learn-
ing to defer trains a model to pass cases to an ex-
pert (Mozannar and Sontag, 2020), and refusal tun-
ing labels questions beyond the model’s knowledge
as ones to decline (Zhang et al., 2024), much as our
hand-off training labels questions a held-out model
answers wrongly. In our experiments both catch
only the errors a model makes itself: like LLMs
that adopt coherent but conflicting context (Xie
et al., 2024), Jev adopts a stale summary, although
it contradicts the log rather than the model’s mem-
ory, and neither mechanism detects it.
7 Conclusion
We asked what Jev cannot decide, and found a
single limit: Jev, like the open Jev-style mod-
els we evaluated, cannot make a decision that an
LLM makes only by writing out intermediate steps.
These models find and select records reliably, but
they fail to track a state among similar records,
follow a precomputed state without checking it,
and lose the real decision items that need reason-
ing, the more so the more reasoning an item needs.
Their confidence flags the errors they make them-
selves but not those they inherit from a wrong input,
and neither an explicit nor a trained hand-off op-
tion closes this gap. One-pass decision models are
therefore best used as fast selectors over a state
that a reasoning model has computed and checked.
Whether training on such decisions, or a few in-
ternal steps before the readout, can move the limit
remains open.
8

Limitations
Our items are isolated decisions: each is posed
once, answered once, and scored once. In a de-
ployed agent loop a decision model is called many
times along a trajectory, its errors compound, and
they interact with the surrounding system, with re-
tries, with memory, and with the state summaries
that earlier steps wrote, the very inputs Section 5
shows it does not check. Measuring these effects re-
quires an end-to-end evaluation of a full agent sys-
tem, which no single-model benchmark provides.
Our results give the per-decision error rates and the
routing signals that such an evaluation would build
on.

A Models and Serving Details
A.1 Decision models
Table 5 lists every model we evaluate. Each model
is served by the code its authors released, behind
an endpoint that accepts a state, a question and an
option list and returns a probability per option; the
two exceptions are the letter-logit readout of plain
Qwen models, which we implement ourselves, and
CLM, which scores options by embedding simi-
larity. All models receive the same items with the
same option order. An input that exceeds a model’s
limit is recorded as unsupported and never trun-
cated: Decider is started with a state limit above
its 32,768-token row limit so that it refuses instead
of cutting, and CLM is run with its truncation dis-
abled (–max-tokens 0). Requests that fail with
HTTP 429, 503 or 529 are retried with exponential
backoff; any other failure is recorded as an error
and excluded from accuracy.
For the open checkpoints we pin the Hugging
Face revision. The revisions behind our num-
bers are 139fdd94 (Kev-4B),2629c06a (Kev-9B),
01b81998 (Kev-27B, the snapshot with a separate
adapter) and27d2d6b8 (JevK5-4B v0.2). Themain
branch of the Kev repositories was replaced on
2026-10-10; the new Kev-9B weights differ from
the ones we report (the same answer on 82% of the
final-state items up to 4k tokens), whereas the new
Kev-4B weights are identical to ours.
Letter-logit readout.For plain Qwen mod-
els (Qwen3.5-4B and 9B, each with its Base
model, and Qwen3.8-27B) we follow the recipe
of jqv (Octalab-Inc, 2026) and SemIf (TheoLeeCJ,
2026). The prompt, in the Qwen chat format with
thinking disabled, contains the document, the ques-
tion and the options labelled A, B, . . . , asks for the
letter only, and ends withAnswer:. The next-token
probabilities of the offered letters, renormalised,
are the answer distribution; nothing is generated
and no temperature is fitted. Items with more than
26 options are unsupported, which removes the
32–64-option selection cells.
Thinking reference.Qwen3.8-27B with think-
ing is served with SGLang and queried through the
OpenAI-compatible chat API with the sampling set-
tings of its model card (temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95,
top-k 20) and at most 24,000 new tokens; a reply
cut off at that limit counts as wrong. The answer
is parsed from the reply, so it has no probabilities
and no AUROC.
A.2 Real-task items and reasoning demand
JevBench items are the public easy, original and
hard splits of the repository (Benchmark Heaven,
2026). Jevals items are rebuilt from the pinned
Hugging Face rows of suite 0.1.0 (Jevals, 2026)
exactly as the Jevals harness builds them, and each
state is checked against the SHA-256 hash in the
suite. Every item is reduced to a closed option
list: yes/no questions get the options yes and no,
with the criteria as their descriptions, and score
questions their levels.
Reasoning demand is measured with Qwen3.8-
27B served by SGLang. The direct readout disables
thinking, forces the reply to begin with Answer:,
and scores each option label by the log-probability
of its tokens followed by the end of the label, nor-
malised over the options; nothing is generated. The
no-thinking and thinking modes draw 10 samples
each with the sampling settings of the model card
(no thinking: temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8, top-k 20,
presence penalty 1.5; thinking: temperature 1.0,
top-p 0.95, top-k 20) and at most 4,096 and 32,768
new tokens; a truncated sample counts as wrong.
The demand of an item is the median number of
thinking tokens over the thinking samples that an-
swer it correctly.

B.1 Controlled benchmark

user. R2 reverses the log and replaces “After the

B.5 Hand-off
Kev-0.8B, trained and evaluated like the larger Kev
models in Table 4, follows the same pattern with
larger effects. Hand-off training lowers accuracy
without the option from 79.3% to 77.9%, raises

58%. The items it answers are then correct 86.5%
of the time, against 91.8% for a threshold on the
untrained model that routes the same share.
