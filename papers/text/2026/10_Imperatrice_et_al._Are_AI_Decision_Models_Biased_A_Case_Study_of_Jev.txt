Are AI Decision Models Biased? A Case Study of Jev
Ilaria Imperatrice Taisia Kameneva
Adam Eric Greenberg Alessandro Romano
Bocconi University
Abstract
Do AI models designed for fast, structured de-
cisions exhibit the cognitive and social biases
observed in human judgment? We evaluate Jev,
a model described as a “System One” decision-
maker, using 41,500 responses across cognitive
reflection tasks, five classical cognitive-bias
paradigms, and a pricing experiment testing eth-
nic and gender bias. At the level of selected an-
swers, the results are largely encouraging. We
confirm previous findings that Jev performs ex-
tremely well on cognitive reflection problems.
We also observe that it generally avoids the
characteristic choice patterns associated with
several well-documented human biases, and
exhibits only small differences among demo-
graphic groups in the tested pricing scenario.
However, this apparent robustness conceals im-
portant vulnerabilities. Its judgments are sen-
sitive to irrelevant numerical information and
the ordering of response options. Moreover,
several cognitive-bias manipulations substan-
tially affect returned probabilities even when
selected answers remain unchanged. Most strik-
ingly, Jev answers a well-known probability
problem correctly in every response but vio-
lates the same underlying probability rule in
every response to an adapted version. We inves-
tigate this discrepancy using 15,000 additional
responses across 30 conjunction tasks. Jev vio-
lates the rule in 54% of responses, with accu-
racy varying dramatically across formulations
and answer orders. These findings suggest that
evaluating decision models solely on familiar
benchmarks and selected answers may substan-
tially overstate their reliability in practical ap-
plications. They also identify vulnerabilities
for developers to address and practical consid-
erations for users integrating such models into
automated decision-making systems.
1 Introduction
Human System One thinking is fast and intuitive,
but also systematically vulnerable to cognitive bi-
ases (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974; Evans and
Stanovich, 2013). Jev is explicitly presented as a
“System One” model designed to make fast judg-
ments (TypeSafe AI, 2026; Almeida, 2026). This
raises a natural question: does a model described
in these terms also display the characteristic biases
of human intuitive judgment?
AI decision models such as Jev are designed
to evaluate a given situation and return structured
judgments, such as choices among predefined alter-
natives, scores, or probabilities, rather than gener-
ate open-ended text. When these structured judg-
ments are consumed directly by software, sensi-
tivity to irrelevant information, framing, or the
presentation of alternatives can affect downstream
decisions. We therefore use classical cognitive-
bias paradigms to test whether Jev’s responses fol-
low patterns observed in human decision making,
whether these patterns extend across task variants,
and whether reversing the order of the answer op-
tions affects Jev’s judgments.
Our evaluation of Jev has three parts. First, cog-
nitive reflection tasks test performance on short
problems designed to elicit an intuitive but incor-
rect answer. Second, five cognitive-bias paradigms
examine anchoring, conjunction judgments, fram-
ing, sunk costs, and zero-risk preferences. Where
applicable, we also test whether changing the or-
der of the response options affects Jev’s judgments,
since human decision makers tend to be suscep-
tible to order effects. Third, a pricing task exam-
ines whether changing a customer’s name, and then
making ethnicity and gender explicit, changes Jev’s
pricing judgment. We do not aggregate the tasks
into a single accuracy or bias score because their
normative benchmarks and response formats differ.
2 Relation to prior work
Psychological experiments have been used to char-
acterize decision-making and reasoning in lan-
guage models (Binz and Schulz, 2023; Coda-Forno

et al., 2024). Related research uses LLMs to simu-
late experimental participants (Aher et al., 2023),
approximate demographic response patterns (Ar-
gyle et al., 2023), and compare chatbot choices
with human behavior in experiments(Mei et al.,
2024; Binz and Schulz, 2023).
Research on cognitive biases documents both
similarities and differences between model and hu-
man judgments. Suri et al. (2024) find anchoring,
conjunction errors, and attribute-framing effects in
GPT-3.5. Hagendorff et al. (2023) show that in-
tuitive errors in cognitive-reflection and semantic-
illusion tasks, observed in earlier models, are sub-
stantially reduced in ChatGPT. Instruction tuning
can also increase susceptibility to some decision
biases, including the certainty effect (Itzhak et al.,
2024). Larger benchmarking frameworks investi-
gate cognitive biases across multiple model fam-
ilies and decision scenarios, alongside interven-
tions intended to reduce them (Echterhoff et al.,
2024; Malberg et al., 2025). Findings on sunk
costs likewise differ across experimental settings.
He and Liu (2025) report little sunk-cost sensitiv-
ity in their evaluation, whereas Chen et al. (2026)
identify sunk-cost effects in mental-accounting ex-
periments, with their strength and direction varying
across models and conditions.
Answer presentation and demographic informa-
tion introduce further sources of variation. Re-
ordering multiple-choice options can change model
responses despite unchanged questions and alter-
natives (Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024; Zheng
et al., 2024). Wang et al. (2024) document
response-order effects in LLM-based evaluation,
while Koo et al. (2024) benchmark broader evalua-
tor biases, including models’ preference for their
own outputs. For demographic bias, BBQ eval-
uates stereotype-based responses in question an-
swering (Parrish et al., 2022), and SoFa examines
disparate treatment across sensitive demographic
groups (Marchiori Manerba et al., 2024). Tamkin
et al. (2023) investigate discriminatory decisions
through controlled changes to explicit demographic
characteristics and implicit cues conveyed through
names.
Recent preprints have begun evaluating Jev and
related specialized decision models. Deußer et al.
(2026) benchmark Jev across 37 datasets, exam-
ining predictive performance and calibration and
reporting unchanged accuracy under option rota-
tion in their MMLU probe. Gao et al. (2026)
identify underuse of ordinal response scales in Jev
and related models that persists after randomiz-
ing candidate positions. Li (2026) compares Jev
with Laya on agent decision tasks and documents
substantial answer-order sensitivity in Laya. Guo
et al. (2026) evaluate Jev as a detector of align-
ment failures, including social bias, in other mod-
els’ outputs. Yang et al. (2026) find that Jev per-
forms very well on cognitive reflection tasks. Our
study complements these evaluations by testing
Jev on classical cognitive-bias paradigms and a
demographic pricing task. We examine whether
patterns associated with human judgment appear
in its responses, whether they extend across task
variants, and whether answer presentation changes
its judgments. Throughout, we distinguish changes
in selected answers from changes in returned prob-
ability scores and confidence.
3 Evaluation setup
3.1 Tasks and experimental manipulations
We evaluate Jev 1.13.0 using 41,500 responses col-
lected between 30 September and 5 October 2026.
Each task supplies a written question or decision
scenario together with response options.
For all cognitive tasks except anchoring, we in-
clude both the original formulation derived from
the seminal study of the relevant bias and an
adapted version. The purpose is to test whether
Jev’s performance extends beyond highly familiar
formulations. Where the original task required an
open-ended response, we constructed closed-ended
response options to accommodate Jev’s structured
output format. In all Choice tasks (Section 3.2), we
test forward and reversed alswer orders.
3.2 Model outputs
Jev is a System One model: a model “built to make
fast, structured decisions that software can use di-
rectly”. It evaluates an input state and “returns
typed decisions and probabilities rather than gen-
erated text” (TypeSafe, 2026). Its probabilities are
trained to be calibrated against outcomes; calibra-
tion is a property of groups of predictions and does
not guarantee that an individual answer is correct.
Questions are posed as one of three primitives,
of which this study uses two: Score and Choice. A
Score is a question type “for rating content against
ordered, descriptive levels”, used when the answer
is a position on a spectrum (TypeSafe, 2026). A
Choice is a question type “for selecting one option
from a defined set”, used when the options have no

4 Cognitive reflection tasks
The cognitive reflection test presents short ques-
tions whose intuitive answer is wrong (Frederick,

2005; Thomson and Oppenheimer, 2016). We use
three classic numerical items (Frederick, 2005),
three verbal items (Thomson and Oppenheimer,
2016), and an adapted version of each verbal item.
We collected 500 responses per item.
Jev chooses the correct option in all 500 re-
sponses for each of the nine items (Table 3). It
is also confident in these answers, with average
confidence exceeding 85% for all items. The intu-
itive wrong answers receive low mean probabilities,
with the highest being 8.96%.
5 Cognitive biases
5.1 Anchoring
Human sentencing judgments can be influenced by
irrelevant numerical anchors (Tversky and Kahne-
man, 1974; Strack and Mussweiler, 1997; Epley
and Gilovich, 2006). In a seminal paper, Englich
et al. (2006) showed that even experienced legal
professionals adjusted sentencing decisions toward
randomly generated and irrelevant anchors. For in-
stance, in one experiment participants themselves
generated the anchor by tossing two dice. We adapt
this idea by preceding a fixed shoplifting scenario
with a single irrelevant numerical cue: “Two dice
were tossed, and their sum was {dice_sum}.” The
sum ranges from 2 to 12 and it is clearly irrelevant
for the task. Jev then evaluates the appropriate sen-
tence on an ordered scale from 3 to 9 months. We
calculate the resulting implied sentence from Jev’s
output . We collected 500 responses for each dice
sum, with the sums randomized across calls.
Changing the irrelevant number changes the
mean implied sentence (Figure 1). From a dice
sum of 5 onward, mean implied sentence gener-
ally increases with the dice sum, while from dice
sum 2 to 4 mean implied sentences decrease. At
dice sums of 3 and 9—the anchor values used in
Study 3 of Englich et al. (2006), where mean sen-
tences increased from 5.28 to 7.81 months, a dif-
ference of 2.53 months—mean implied sentences
were 6.248 and 6.550 months, respectively, a dif-
ference of 0.302 months (simultaneous 95% CI:
[0.287, 0.318]). Across the full range, mean im-
plied sentences increased from 6.338 months at
dice sum 2 to 6.811 months at dice sum 12, a dif-
ference of 0.473 months (simultaneous 95% CI:
[0.454, 0.492]). These confidence intervals use a
normal approximation with a Bonferroni correction
across all 55 pairwise comparisons. Both contrasts
are directionally consistent with the human finding
of higher sentences following the higher anchor.
Mean returned confidence was low across all dice
sums, reaching a maximum of 17.6% at dice sum 7
and falling to 0.8% at dice sum 12.
5.2 Conjunction judgments
The conjunction fallacy occurs when people judge
two events occurring together as more probable
than one of those events occurring alone, even
though this is mathematically impossible (Tver-
sky and Kahneman, 1983; Wells, 1985; Morier and
Borgida, 1984). We examine this bias using an
original and an edited version of the task. In both
versions, the model must assess which of two state-
ments is more probable: that a person holds a par-
ticular occupation, or that the same person holds
that occupation and belongs to an activist group.
The first statement is necessarily at least as proba-
ble as the second. Each version is tested with both
answer orders, collecting 250 responses per order.
The difference between the original and edited
versions is striking (Figure 2). In the original ver-
sion, Jev chooses the correct answer in all 500
responses, whereas in the edited version, it chooses
the incorrect answer in all 500 responses. Re-
versing the answer order changes the mean re-
turned probabilities assigned to the options but
never the chosen answer. In the original version,
the mean probability assigned to the correct answer
is 93.60% when it appears first and 86.51% when
it appears second, a −7.10 percentage points dif-
ference (95% CI: [−7.57,−6.62] ). In the edited
version, the corresponding mean probabilities are
2.85% and 2.20%, a −0.65 percentage points dif-
ference (95% CI: [−0.77,−0.53] ). Despite consis-
tently choosing the incorrect answer, the model’s
mean returned confidence in the edited version
remains high under both orders, at 94.28% and
95.42%, respectively.
Thus, correct performance on the canonical item
does not extend to this adaptation.

become more willing to take risks when they are
framed as losses (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981;
McNeil et al., 1982; Levin et al., 1998). We ex-

5.4 Sunk costs
The sunk-cost fallacy describes the tendency to
continue a project because resources have already
been spent, even though the decision should depend
on future costs and benefits (Arkes and Blumer,
1985; Staw, 1976; Garland, 1990).
We test this tendency using the original task from
Arkes and Blumer (1985) and an edited version. In
both versions, we compare Jev’s willingness to
invest with and without prior investment.
All 2,000 responses reject investment. Never-
theless, the sunk-cost condition increases the mean
probability assigned to investing in both the orig-
inal and edited versions, regardless of answer or-
der. In the original version, the mean probability
rises from 1.00% without prior investment to 7.12%
when “No” appears first and 5.92% when “Yes”
appears first, increases of 6.12 percentage points
(95% CI [6.01, 6.23]) and 4.92 percentage points
(95% CI [4.83, 5.01]), respectively. In the edited
version, the mean probability rises from 0.00% to
9.16% and 9.39%, respectively, with corresponding
95% CIs for the increases of [8.91, 9.41] and [9.12,
9.66].

6 Conjunction: Robustness
To examine whether the contrasting results of
the original and edited conjunction tasks reflect
a broader pattern, we constructed 30 additional
scenarios varying in domain, plausibility of the ad-
ditional event, wording, grammatical quality, and
language. As in Section 5.2, each scenario was
tested with both answer orders, collecting 250 re-
sponses per order (500 per scenario).
Across the 30 scenarios (15,000 responses), Jev
selected the incorrect answer in 53.96% of re-
sponses. Performance varied substantially across
scenarios: nine yielded exclusively incorrect re-
sponses, whereas eleven yielded exclusively cor-
rect responses. Accuracy also varied with answer
order, averaging 39.79% when the conjunction was
presented first and 52.29% when it was presented
second. In two scenarios, accuracy ranged from 0%
to 100% across answer orders. These descriptive
findings indicate that Jev’s difficulties in apply-
ing the conjunction rule extend beyond the single
edited task and arise across diverse formulations,
while highlighting substantial variation across sce-
narios and sensitivity to answer presentation.

7 Social bias
7.1 Design and outcome
The social-bias experiment tests whether the price
recommended to an otherwise identical car buyer
changes with the buyer’s race or gender (Ayres and
Siegelman, 1995). We ask Jev to recommend an ini-
tial offer price for a car buyer, varying the buyer’s
name while keeping all other stated customer and
vehicle characteristics fixed. The model evaluates
ten price options ranging from $28,750 to $30,895,
presented in ascending order. The customer is a
30-year-old bank systems analyst living in an af-
fluent Chicago neighborhood, with independent
financing.
The experiment uses 125 names assigned to six
demographic groups: White, Black, and Hispanic
women and men. Five groups contain 21 names
each; the Black women group contains 20. Name
selection draws on published name-validation stud-
ies with US respondents (Gaddis, 2017a,b; Baert
et al., 2022). Each name is tested in two conditions.
In the name-only condition, the customer’s name
is provided without explicit demographic labels. In
the explicit condition, the same name is accompa-
nied by its corresponding ethnic and gender labels,
such as “a Hispanic man.”
We collect 100 responses per name in each con-
dition, yielding 12,500 responses per condition and
25,000 responses overall. The sequence of names
is randomized within each run.

8 Discussion
Classic bias experiments are influential because
they reveal recurring patterns of judgment whose
relevance extends beyond the scenarios used to
demonstrate them. Decisions that look very differ-
ent can share the same susceptibility to irrelevant
information, context, or changes in presentation.
For a model designed to supply fast, low-cost judg-
ments, these tests help identify both opportunities
for improvement and conditions under which its
capabilities can be used effectively.
In several respects, Jev’s performance on these
tests is encouraging. Jev answered all nine cog-
nitive reflection items correctly in every call and
rejected investment in every sunk-cost condition.
It generally favored greater harm reduction in the
zero-risk tasks. In the pricing experiment, demo-
graphic differences were small and the highest-
scored price was almost unchanged. These suc-
cesses support the practical promise of a model
designed to make inexpensive, rapid judgments.
Jev nevertheless gave the wrong answer in ev-
ery call on the edited conjunction task. Its perfect
performance on the original conjunction task there-
fore did not extend to the edited version. The con-
junction experiment raises a broader concern about
evaluations based on well-known benchmarks. Al-
though this does not establish that the model has
memorized the original example, it suggests that
successful performance on familiar benchmarks
may substantially overstate the model’s ability to
generalize to unfamiliar instances of the same prob-
lem.
Two further findings have implications across
applications. In the anchoring experiment, the irrel-
evant dice value affected the sentencing estimate
despite unchanged case facts. Users may reason-
ably assume that adding background information
unrelated to a decision is harmless, since the model
can simply disregard it. These results challenge that
assumption. Evaluation should therefore test sensi-
tivity to irrelevant context, including the incidental
information likely to accompany real requests.
Answer order creates a related problem. For ex-
ample, reversing the answer options list reduced
selection of the program producing the larger ab-
solute risk reduction from 99.6% to 65.6% in the
edited zero-risk task, and increased selection of the
zero-risk program from 0.4% to 34.4%.
The probabilities assigned to the answer options
also matter when the chosen answers remain stable.
For instance, consider a customer-service system
that handles a request automatically when the prob-
ability assigned to that option is at least 80%, and
otherwise escalates it to a human. A shift from
83% to 77% changes the application’s action even
though automatic handling remains the model’s pre-
ferred answer. Most biases had an impact on proba-
bility scores. For example, the sunk-cost condition
and framing changed the probability assigned to
the investment and risky option, respectively.
