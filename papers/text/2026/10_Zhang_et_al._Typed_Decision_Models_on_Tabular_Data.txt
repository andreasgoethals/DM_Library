Abstract
Typed decision models are language models
that answer a question with a fixed set of possi-
ble answers by returning a probability for each
answer instead of generating text. We study
them on tabular data and compare four deci-
sion models with three large language models
(LLMs) on row classification, fact verification
and anomaly detection. Every request is sent in
its original form and under 21 types of rewrite
that keep its content, such as renaming the op-
tions or writing the table in another format. The
decision models perform as well as the LLMs
at a lower cost. Three of them are also far more
robust to how the question is formulated. No
model we test is robust to how the table is orga-
nized. For every model, at least a third of the
row decisions change under some rewrite of the
table or the question. The decisions to which
a decision model gives low probability are the
ones most likely to change. Decision models
are a practical choice for tabular decisions, but
their robustness remains an open problem.
1 Introduction
Tabular data, in which each row describes a sample
and each column one of its heterogeneous features,
is one of the most common forms of data in fields
such as healthcare, finance and network security
(Borisov et al., 2022; Fang et al., 2024). On such
data, gradient-boosted trees (Chen and Guestrin,
2016; Ke et al., 2017; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018)
still outperform most deep networks designed for
tables (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Borisov et al., 2022),
and tabular foundation models do best on small
tables (Hollmann et al., 2025; Prior Labs, 2026).
Large language models (LLMs) have opened
a new route for tabular data (Fang et al., 2024).
LLM-based methods first serialize a row or a ta-
ble into text, for example as feature–value pairs
or as a Markdown table (Hegselmann et al., 2023;
* Corresponding author.
Sui et al., 2024). The serialized row is then clas-
sified by prompting an LLM with a description
of the task and a few in-context examples, or by
fine-tuning it (Dinh et al., 2022; Hegselmann et al.,
2023). The LLM can also turn the labelled rows
into features and rules for a conventional model
(Han et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025). This works well
even when only a few labelled rows are available
(Hegselmann et al., 2023). However, LLMs are not
built for decisions. The LLM-generated output has
to be mapped to one of the options, and every deci-
sion needs a full decoding process, which can be
expensive when a large number of decisions need
to be made. Their answers can also change with
the order of the columns, the format of the rows or
the wording of the question (Sui et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2024).
Recently, a new class of language models has
been built for such decisions:typed decision mod-
els(decision models for short). TypeSafe released
the first of them, Jev (TypeSafe, 2026). Jev takes a
sample or a table together with one or more ques-
tions, each with a fixed set of possible answers such
as the classes of a task. Instead of generating text,
it returns a probability for every answer of every
question in a single request. TypeSafe trains it with
Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions
(RLCD) so that these probabilities match outcomes,
and reports that its confidence is calibrated and
that similar inputs receive similar answers (Type-
Safe, 2026). Open-weight decision models such as
Clef and Kev-27B followed, trained from an open-
weight LLM with proper scoring rules (Cloudflare,
2026; Palmer, 2026). OpenAI now also offers GPT-
6 Luna Decisions, a decision model built on its
LLM GPT-6 Luna, through its Decisions API (Ope-
nAI, 2026a). Decision models thus avoid genera-
tion and decoding, since every decision comes with
a probability in a single request. Whether they are
also robust to how the table and the question are
written is open, since a decision model also reads

Of the 112 arXiv papers on decision models pub-
lished in the three weeks after the release of Jev,
none studies them as a method for tabular data (Ta-
ble 11). For LLMs on tables, decisions are known
to depend on the serialization (Sui et al., 2024;
Hegselmann et al., 2023), on the order of rows and
columns (Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024) and on
the in-context examples (Hegselmann et al., 2023).
These questions have not been asked of decision
models.
To study them in depth, we change how each
request is written without changing the information
the decision needs, for example by renaming the
options or writing the table in another format. We
call such a change arewrite. We send every request
in its original form and in each rewritten form to
decision models and LLMs.
Our research questions are as follows:
RQ1 Are decision models practical for tabular de-
cisions?
RQ2 Are they robust to the formulation of the
question, such as the names and order of
the options?
RQ3 Are they robust to how the table is organized,
such as its format and the order of its rows
and columns?
RQ4 Does their confidence indicate which deci-
sions are sensitive to rewrites?
We evaluate seven models—four decision models
(Jev, Clef, Kev-27B and GPT-6 Luna Decisions)
and three LLMs (GPT-6 Luna, Qwen3.8-27B and
Llama-3.3-70B)—across three tabular tasks cover-
ing row classification, fact verification and anomaly
detection, under 21 types of rewrite. Clef and Kev-
27B are trained from Qwen3.8-27B, which lets us
compare the two ways of deciding on the same
backbone (Section 2).
Our main findings are as follows:
Decision models perform as well as LLMs on
these tasks and are cheaper to run (Table 1). They
are also far less affected by how the question is
formulated. In contrast, GPT-6 Luna Decisions is
as sensitive to the order of the options as the LLMs.
Writing the table differently, however, changes
many decisions of every model, even though accu-
racy stays almost the same. The decisions that a
decision model gives low probability are the ones
that change most often.
2 Experimental setup
2.1 Decision interfaces
We refer to a model’s output for one item as adeci-
sion: the class of a sample, the verdict on a claim
about a table, or whether a sample is anomalous.
Arequestis one call to a model and may contain
several items. A decision model receives a request
as astate, such as a group of samples or a table,
with one question per item whose possible answers
are fixed in advance. It returns a probability for
every option of every question in a single request
(TypeSafe, 2026). An LLM receives a prompt built
from the same request and generates text, which we
map to one of the options. Its confidence has to be
read from that text, from its token probabilities or
from repeated draws (Li and Wagle, 2026; Deußer
et al., 2026).

In row classification, a decision-model request
contains the 16 rows of a group as separate typed
questions over a shared state, whereas an LLM
prompt contains one row, as in TabLLM. In fact
verification and anomaly detection, every model
receives the same claim or the same batch of 15
samples. All decision models receive identical re-
quests, and the LLM prompts are built from them.
2.3 Evaluation
We evaluate row classification by Macro-F1, fact
verification by accuracy and anomaly detection by
AUROC, each averaged over datasets or sources
(Table 2). We also report the latency and price of
each decision, and for RQ1 we vary the number of
in-context examples from none to as many as fit.
Besides its original form, we send every request
under rewrites, which change how it is written
but keep all the information the decision needs
(Figure 1). Rewrites of the question serve RQ2,
rewrites of the table serve RQ3, and each task uses
those of the 21 types of rewrite that apply to it
(Table 12). We checked that each rewrite keeps
every cell and header by recovering the original
request from it. The one exception is the order of
rows on TabFact, which can change the truth of
claims about positions such as “first”. We leave
these claims out of RQ3 and RQ4.
A decisionflipswhen it differs from the decision
under the original request, and an invalid output
counts as a flip. Even an identical request can re-
ceive a different decision. We therefore report each
model’sre-send rate, the share of its decisions that
flip when the identical request is sent again, and
compare the flips under rewrites with it. Section 6
defines confidence, and Appendix C gives every
result with intervals.

rewrite of the table changes (Section 5). We call
such a decisionfragile, meaning that at least one

stable. We ask whether a model’s confidence in-

table can hold many rows, such a signal is most
useful if it comes with each decision rather than
from repeated requests.

Re-querying the least confident rows.Since
low confidence marks the decisions that a rewrite
is likely to change, it can also tell which rows are
worth asking again. On row classification, we send
a share of the rows again in the four other formats
of Table 12 and combine the five answers, by the
highest mean probability for a decision model and
by majority vote for an LLM. Re-querying every
row raises Macro-F1 by 2.0 to 6.4 points for six of
the models but takes four more requests per row.

takes one more request per row on average and re-
covers 79–89% of the gain for the decision models
and 64–86% for the LLMs, against 23–44% for ran-
dom rows (Figure 4). Most of this gain comes from
leaving the original array of values, the weakest for-
mat for six of the seven models, and the best other
format alone comes within 0.2 points of the com-
bination or exceeds it. The rows whose decisions
change with the format are thus mostly the least
confident ones, and re-querying them undoes most
of the effect of a weak format. Kev-27B, whose
weakest format is not the array, loses 0.7 points
(not shown).
Confidence follows stability more than correct-
ness.Confident decisions are more often right.
Among the 25% of decisions with the highest con-
fidence, the decision models are right 67–99% of
the time, against 43–73% among the 25% with the
lowest. Yet their confidence separates stable from
fragile decisions far better than right from wrong
ones (Table 19). On row classification and anomaly
detection, stable but wrong decisions carry almost
as much confidence as stable and right ones (0.67–
0.86 against 0.76–0.90) and far more than frag-
ile ones (0.54–0.66). On TabFact the order is the
same, with smaller gaps. Such stable errors make
up 17–23% of the decisions in row classification
and anomaly detection and 4–5% on TabFact, and
no signal of instability can reveal them (cf. Ganesh
et al., 2026). Llama-3.3-70B’s confidence is high
in all four groups (0.85–1.00).
Why confidence predicts flips.A decision flips
when a rewrite moves itsmargin, the gap between
the log-probabilities of the chosen option and the
best other option, below zero (cf. Cohen et al.,
2019; Bhojanapalli et al., 2021). For the decision
models, the flip rates predicted from the margin
alone, assuming a small shift that is nearly indepen-
dent of the item, match the observed ones decile
by decile (Figure 5). Such a shift flips only border-
line decisions, which would explain why the flips
cancel in accuracy and why the confidence predicts
them.
♂lightbulbTakeaway.A decision model’s probability indicates
which of its decisions a rewrite is likely to change. The
confidence of an LLM is a weaker indicator. Re-querying
only the least confident rows recovers most of the gain of
re-querying every row.
7 Related work
LLMs on tables.LLMs can classify rows se-
rialized as text from a few labelled examples, by
prompting or fine-tuning (Dinh et al., 2022; Hegsel-
mann et al., 2023). They can also build features and

rules for a conventional model (Han et al., 2024;
Ye et al., 2025). LLMs also detect anomalous sam-
ples (Tsai et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2026; Ye et al.,
2026) and verify claims about tables on TabFact
(Chen et al., 2020), and Fang et al. (2024) survey
the field. Each of these methods addresses one kind
of task and reports accuracy. We compare LLMs
with decision models on three kinds and look at
single decisions.
Robustness of LLMs.LLM answers change
with the order, names and format of the options and
with the wording of the prompt (Zhao et al., 2021;
Zheng et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2024; Mizrahi et al.,
2024). On tables, the serialization changes results
(Sui et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2024), and reorder-
ing or transposing a table lowers accuracy on table
question answering (Liu et al., 2024; Zhao et al.,
2023; Zhou et al., 2024). For single predictions,
Hamman et al. (2025) find that the probability of
tabular LLMs does not reveal their inconsistency
across fine-tuning seeds, and Ganesh et al. (2026)
find that an LLM’s uncertainty identifies prompt-
sensitive items but not wrong ones. We measure
which individual decisions rewrites of the table
change, for LLMs and for models that return a
probability by design.
Decision models.Early studies measure Jev’s
accuracy and calibration on text (Deußer et al.,
2026), its consistency under benign and injected
edits (Hu et al., 2026) and the readout stability of
open-weight decision models (Li and Chen, 2026).
Others apply decision models to single tabular ap-
plications (Severo et al., 2026; Jung, 2026), and an
audit lists the missing tests (Tang and Zheng, 2026).
We compare decision models, including one built
on an LLM, with LLMs across tabular tasks.
8 Discussion
When to use decision models?Decision mod-
els suit tabular decisions whose answers form a
fixed set, such as classifying rows, verifying claims
or flagging anomalies. In tasks with few labelled
rows, they perform as well as LLMs, cost less per
decision, respond faster and return a useful con-
fidence with each decision (Section 3). They are
most useful when many rows need a decision, when
the wording of the question may vary and when un-
certain decisions need to be identified (Sections 4
and 6). They are less suited when many labelled
rows are available. From around 64 labelled rows
on, tabular foundation models and trained models
perform better (Section 3), and decision models
have shorter context windows than LLMs, which
limits how many labelled rows they can read. By
design they return only one of a fixed set of choices,
so they do not cover tasks that need free text, such
as an explanation or a computed value.
When to use LLMs?LLMs suit the task better
when the answer has to be written in tokens rather
than chosen from a fixed set, for example when
a decision must be explained or an open question
about a table answered. A strong LLM is also a
reasonable starting point when almost no labelled
rows exist (Section 3). When an LLM is used for
decisions with a fixed set of answers, a single ques-
tion prompt may not be relied on, since presenting
the options in several orders and aggregating the
answers can reduce its dependence (Zheng et al.,
2024).
Future directions.No model we test is robust
to how the table is organized, and this is the main
limitation of decision models on tables. A natural
next step is to train decision models on rewrites
of the table. In previous work, LLMs have been
trained to predict on unseen tables in few shots
more accurately than XGBoost and TabPFN (Gard-
ner et al., 2024). With RLCD, decision models
can also follow this path and be trained to improve
their decision accuracy and robustness on tabular
tasks. LLMs and decision models can also work
together: a decision model can make most of the
decisions, and an LLM can explain or review the
few it is least confident about. Systems that com-
bine the two kinds of model in this way are another
direction for future work.
9 Conclusion
We studied typed decision models as a method for
decisions on tabular data. On row classification,
fact verification and anomaly detection, they per-
form as well as LLMs at a fraction of the cost.
Except for GPT-6 Luna Decisions, renaming or re-
ordering the options barely changes their decisions.
How the table is organized, however, affects deci-
sion models and LLMs alike, and about a third of
the row decisions change under some rewrite of
the table. The decisions to which a decision model
gives low probability are the ones most likely to
change. Whether decision models can be made
robust to how the table is organized remains open.

Limitations
First, each of the seven models is tested in one ver-
sion. Jev and GPT-6 Luna Decisions are closed ser-
vices whose internal processing is not visible to us
and may change across versions, and the Decisions
API is still in public beta. The two open-weight
decision models reproduce the findings we rely on,
but other decision models, and later versions of the
ones we test, may behave differently.
Second, we cover three kinds of tabular deci-
sions and use subsets of each benchmark, since
every request is sent under every rewrite to seven
models. The 21 types of rewrite cover the format
of the table, the order of its rows and columns and
the formulation of the question. They do not cover
other changes that keep the content, such as renam-
ing columns or converting units, so our results say
little about robustness to those changes.
