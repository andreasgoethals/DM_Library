Releasing the Inner Jev: A Simple Recipe for Open Decision Models
Jonathan Geuter * 1 2 Andrew Cheng * 1 David Alvarez-Melis 1 2
Abstract
LLM-based decision-making systems, also called
System One models, have recently gained popu-
larity, most notably through Jev (Almeida, 2026).
Given a typed input state, a question, and a set
of options, they return a probability for each op-
tion in a single forward pass, without text gen-
eration. We show that existing open instruction-
tuned language models can achieve comparable
performancewithout any additional training, by
reading out answers from letters (A, B, C, etc.) in
the model’s output logits at the last token position.
Furthermore, we show how a simple supervised
fine-tuning recipe can improve their performance
further. We evaluate four open instruction-tuned
language models: Gemma 4 31B, Gemma 4 12B,
Qwen3.6-27B, and Qwen3.5-4B. We fine-tune
each using LoRA and a decision head initialized
from a slice of the language model head. Further-
more, we introduceOpenDecisions, a novel 2,352-
question validation dataset designed to reflect the
task composition of JevBench (JevBench main-
tainers, 2026) and which computes a Capability
score based on accuracy and calibration metrics.
Without fine-tuning, Gemma 4 31B scores 79.6
on OpenDecisions, compared to Jev’s 80.1. After
undergoing our finetuning recipe, it scores 84.4 on
OpenDecisions and 81.5 on JevBench v1.7, out-
performing Jev by 4.3 and 4.4 Capability points
respectively and setting a new state-of-the-art on
OpenDecisions. On a suite of public decision
datasets, our models achieve accuracy compara-
ble to Jev, with lower calibration error. Our results
show that open language models already possess
System One capabilities, and with simple fine-
tuning they outperform Jev on a wide range of
decision tasks. We release our models, serving
code, and OpenDecisions.

1. Introduction
Many applications require choosing among a fixed set of
options, such as routing a support ticket, checking a request
against a policy, or picking the right tool in an agentic work-
flow. Language models can make these decisions by gen-
erating text, but generating tokens sequentially introduces
additional inference costs and latency. For tasks requiring
only a choice and its associated probabilities, a single for-
ward pass avoids this overhead and provides uncertainty
estimates that downstream systems can use directly.
Using a pretrained language model with a classification
head has long been well-established in the literature, in-
cluding ULMFiT (Howard & Ruder, 2018), GPT (Radford
et al., 2018), and BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). Jev (Almeida,
2026) adopts this approach: given a state, a question, and
a set of options, it returns a probability for each option.
TypeSafe, the company behind Jev, describes it as using
a specialized architecture, a parallel sampler, and a train-
ing method called Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated
Decisions (RLCD) (Almeida, 2026). Its weights and train-
ing recipe are not public. The format has attracted several
open implementations, which are compared on the JevBench
leaderboard (JevBench maintainers, 2026).
Are specialized architectures or training methods neces-
sary for competitive decision models?We find that a sim-
ple approach using open-source language models already
achieves performance comparable to Jev. When given
a multiple-choice question, we extract probabilities over
the options from the model’s logits (see Section 4 for de-
tails), an established approach to evaluating multiple-choice
models (Hendrycks et al., 2021a; Robinson & Wingate,
2023), and apply simple temperature scaling for calibra-
tion. Without further fine-tuning, Gemma 4 31B (Gemma
Team, Google DeepMind, 2026) and Qwen3.6-27B (Qwen
Team, 2026b) achieve Capability scores of 79.6 and 79.0
on our validation setOpenDecisionsrespectively, compared
with 80.1 for Jev. Both models also achieve similar aver-
age accuracy to Jev across seven publicly available decision
datasets.

improve performance further. We add LoRA adapters to
the language model and initialize a decision head from the

2. Background and related work
Jev-style decision models.A Jev-style decision consists
of a state (text or JSON), a question, andK possible options.
JevBench considers three types of questions:choice(pick
one of K options),yes/no(do the given criteria hold for the
state?), andscore(pick one of several ordered levels). For
yes/no questions, the prediction is represented by a single
probability P(yes) . These probabilities allow downstream
systems not only to select an answer, but also to return a
confidence associated with it. For this to be reliable, the
predicted probabilities should be well-calibrated.
Classification with pretrained LLMs.LLM classifica-
tion predates Jev: ULMFiT adds a classification head to a
pretrained recurrent language model, while GPT and BERT
adapt pretrained transformers using supervised output lay-
ers (Howard & Ruder, 2018; Radford et al., 2018; Devlin
et al., 2019). PET (Schick & Sch¨utze, 2021) uses cloze-style
prompts to construct soft labels and then trains a conven-
tional sequence classifier with a classification head. A sepa-
rate line of work handles labels supplied in natural language:
Yin et al. (2019) formulate zero-shot classification as en-
tailment between the input and candidate label hypotheses,
while GLiClass (Stepanov et al., 2025) jointly encodes the
input and candidate labels and scores them in a single pass.
Open decision models.Several open systems implement
this format using existing language models. Some re-
quire no additional training and obtain probabilities di-
rectly from answer-letter logits, including SemIf (SemIf
contributors, 2026), open-alternative-jev (IkerMoel, 2026),
Cygnet (blockbrain-ai, 2026) and deck31b (krishna-
gogineni-765, 2026); others average over option or-
ders (Zhang et al., 2026) or score each option in its
own prompt (Yinsongxu, 2026). Fine-tuned systems
include Plumb (crh225, 2026), JevK5 (allebee, 2026),
Hopper (HopitAI, 2026) and reflex (kshetrajna12, 2026)
(LoRA on Qwen3.5-4B), decider (Marosi, 2026) (full fine-

Concurrent work.Li & Wagle (2026) also study whether
existing language models can perform Jev-style decisions
without additional training. Rather than using answer letters,
they extract probabilities from numbered options using a
partially completed answer prompt. They find that an un-
modified Qwen3.5-4B performs comparably to other deci-
sion models built on the same backbone, which corroborates
our zero-shot finding. They also study fine-tuning 4B and
0.6B models with a listwise loss with KL regularization. We
compare their number readout with our letter readout on
OpenDecisions and find similar zero-shot Capability scores
for Gemma 4 31B (80.5 for numbered options and 79.6 for
answer letters). Since the number readout requires multiple
forward passes (each digit requires a forward pass, plus one
to denote the end of the number), whereas our letter readout
requires only one, we adopt the latter.
Calibration.Correct predictions alone are not enough
when downstream systems rely on the reported probabilities.
Neural networks are often overconfident, and temperature
scaling provides a simple way to improve calibration with-
out changing the preferred answer (Guo et al., 2017; Desai
& Durrett, 2020). Previous work shows that pretrained
language models can produce reasonably calibrated prob-
abilities, while instruction tuning can increase overconfi-
dence (Kadavath et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023). Predictions
can also depend on the order and labels of the answer op-
tions (Zheng et al., 2024; Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2023).
In this work, we show that the model output probabilities
themselves already lead to better calibration than Jev (see
Section 5). We leave more sophisticated calibration ap-
proaches for future work.

(i) enough questions to reliably compare their performance
and (ii) sufficient coverage of the tasks they are designed
to solve. Existing public datasets have limitations in both
respects. JevBench has released only 231 questions, which
we call JevBench-Open. Other public decision datasets,
such as those from SemIf and TypeSafe, contain only 100
to 350 questions as well. Although larger multiple-choice
benchmarks exist, they primarily test general knowledge

Validity.14 public decision models come out on Open-

Scoring.We score a system by Capability= (I+C)/2 ,
adapted from JevBench’s scorer without its abstention band
for yes/no answers. Intelligence I is chance-corrected accu-
racy, 100 (acc−chance)/(1−chance) , computed per ques-
tion type and difficulty tier and averaged with tier weights
0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.4 (easy to hard) and equal type weights.
A yes/no answer counts as correct when its more likely
side is correct. Calibration C averages one score per type:
100 (1−ECE/0.5) (Pakdaman Naeini et al., 2015) of the
top answer on hard choice items, of P(yes) on yes/no items,
and for score items the mean of the top-answer ECE score
and 100 (1−RPS) , the ranked probability score (Epstein,
1969). We report 95% intervals as value ± 1.96 SE. The
standard error SE combines question sampling (a paired
bootstrap over question groups, 1,000 resamples, the same
for all systems) and, for trained models, the variation be-
tween training seeds.
4. A simple recipe
In this section, we explain how decision models work in de-
tail, and give a simple training recipe to apply on pretrained
LLMs to improve their decision-making capabilities.
Letter readout.Each sample is presented to the model
with options labeled A, B, C, and so on. Following
SemIf (SemIf contributors, 2026), we use a JSON message
containing the state (evidence), question (criterion),
and answer options. The model is instructed to select one
answer letter, using its standard chat template with thinking
disabled (see Appendix A for details).
Rather than generating a textual answer, we extract the
model’s logits for the answer letters after a single forward
pass. Let h∈R d denote the final hidden state at the last
prompt position, after the final normalization layer, and let
W0 ∈R 16×d contain the rows of the language-model head
corresponding to the letters A through P. For a question
with K options, we obtain the probability of option k by
applying a softmax over theseKlogits:

For Gemma, we preserve the model’s original final-logit
soft-capping by replacing W0h with ctanh(W 0h/c) with
c= 30 . Without soft-capping, the decision head is a single
affine function of h. After training, the LoRA adapter can
be merged into the pretrained weights, so inference requires
only one forward pass.
Calibration.The probabilities produced by language
models are not always well calibrated (Guo et al., 2017;
Desai & Durrett, 2020; Tian et al., 2023). In our exper-
iments, Gemma’s probabilities are particularly overconfi-
dent. We address this using temperature scaling (Guo et al.,
2017), which adjusts the predicted probabilities without
changing the preferred answer. We fit one temperature for
each question type by minimizing negative log-likelihood
on 3,884 held-out questions drawn from the training distribu-
tion. These questions use the same format as OpenDecisions,
but no OpenDecisions questions are used for calibration. We
note that this is a very simple calibration strategy, and more
sophisticated calibration approaches, such as directly incor-
porating calibration in the training loss, could likely help.
One challenge is that the calibration data differ from Open-
Decisions. As a result, the fitted temperatures do not always
transfer well. For the trained Qwen models, temperatures
below one sharpen the predictions and reduce performance
on OpenDecisions. We therefore restrict temperatures to be
at least one, allowing calibration to reduce confidence but
not increase it (Appendix G).

Training.We fine-tune the models using supervised learn-
ing, keeping the original language-model weights frozen.
Specifically, we add rank-32 LoRA adapters (α= 64 , no
dropout) to all attention and MLP projections, including the
linear-attention projections in the Qwen models, and train
them jointly with the decision head. We minimize cross-
entropy against the target probability distribution when avail-
able, or the correct answer otherwise. We also shuffle the
order of the answer options during training.

at step 400. Each step processes 65,536 padded tokens,

5. Results
Setup.We evaluate four open models: Gemma 4 31B and
12B (Gemma Team, Google DeepMind, 2026), Qwen3.6-
27B (Qwen Team, 2026b), and Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team,
2026a), both before and after fine-tuning (three training
seeds per model). We compare them with Jev, accessed
through its API, and 13 public systems using their authors’
implementations. All systems are evaluated on the same
questions in OpenDecisions. For each public system, we
report the better of its original predictions and predictions
recalibrated using our separate calibration set, based on
its OpenDecisions score. This favors the competing sys-
tems. Since we selected our training recipe using OpenDeci-
sions, we also evaluate on seven held-out decision datasets,
JevBench-Open (a small 231-sample public dataset released
by JevBench), and six standard multiple-choice benchmarks.
5.1. LLMs as zero-shot decision models
We find that strong open language models already achieve
performance comparable to Jev without fine-tuning. On

OpenDecisions, Gemma 4 31B achieves a Capability score
of 79.6 ( I= 71.9 , C= 87.4 ) and Qwen3.6-27B scores
79.0, compared with 80.1 for Jev (Figure 2, Table 3). Both
models also achieve similar average accuracy to Jev across
seven publicly available decision datasets (Section 5.3).
Calibration plays an important role in this result. Gemma’s
raw answer-letter probabilities are overconfident, and tem-
perature scaling raises its Capability score from 72.9 to
79.6 without changing its preferred answers. The fitted tem-
peratures are 6.0 for choice questions and 7.2 for yes/no
questions (Appendix G). The answer-letter readout also dis-
cards very little probability mass: Gemma 4 31B assigns
99.99% of its next-token probability to the available answer
letters, and every backbone we evaluate assigns over 99.4%
on average (Appendix H).
Performance also depends on the underlying model. The
smaller Gemma 4 12B and Qwen3.5-4B models score 75.4
and 63.6 on OpenDecisions, respectively. On the public
decision datasets, zero-shot accuracy increases rapidly with
model size up to 4B parameters, and approaches Jev’s level
between 12B and 31B (Appendix E). The Gemma 4 31B
base model evaluated using a plain completion prompt
scores only 64.0, while the instruction-tuned model scores
78.9 with the same prompt (Appendix H). Instruction tuning
therefore matters, and our training does not replace it: the
base model reaches only 70.3 after training with our recipe
(Section 5.4).
5.2. A short training run goes beyond Jev
Our LoRA training raises Gemma 4 31B from 79.6 to 84.4
Capability on OpenDecisions, a gain of 4.8 ± 2.0. The
result is the highest OpenDecisions score of all systems
we evaluated (Table 3), leading Jev by 4.3 ± 2.5, Eikos-
27B by 1.3 ± 2.0, deck31b by 3.1 ± 1.9, Quyet-1.0-Large
by 2.5 ± 2.1, and AutoJev-27B by 3.9 ± 2.4. Our trained
model has the best Calibration of all systems (92.3); Quyet-
1.0-Large has a slightly higher Intelligence (76.7 vs. 76.4)
but lower Calibration (86.9).
The smaller models gain less from training than the 31B:
With the same recipe, Qwen3.6-27B gains 2.2 ± 2.2 and
Gemma 4 12B −0.4 ± 1.8. On Qwen3.5-4B the recipe
does not help (63.0 vs. 63.6 zero-shot); with the three
changes for this model (Section 4) it gains 3.0 ± 2.6. For
Gemma 4 12B, longer training does not gain beyond noise
either: up to 3,000 steps it scores between 75.0 and 76.3,
against 75.4 zero-shot (Table 9). Training does, however,
make its temperatures unnecessary: the raw readout (no
temperature scaling) rises from 65.4 to 74.0 (Appendix G).
Training raises Intelligence for every model (27B +5.0,
12B +1.4, 4B +6.8), but it lowers Calibration for these
three (27B −0.6, 12B −2.2, 4B −0.8), which cancels part
of the gain. Two reasons may contribute: we chose the
recipe on the 31B model, and part of the teacher labels
come from Qwen3.6-27B itself. On the standard multiple-
choice benchmarks, training does raise the accuracy of every
model (Section 5.3).
We release our trained 31B as Jeff-1.0-Large, and the
other models as Jeff-1.0-Qwen-27B, Jeff-1.0-Medium
(Gemma 4 12B), and Jeff-1.0-Qwen-4B.
5.3. Public evaluation sets
OpenDecisions was built by us and we used it to select
our training recipe. We also evaluate our models on pub-
lic datasets to assess whether the improvements generalize
beyond OpenDecisions.
Held-out decision datasets.Table 4 reports accuracy and
calibration on seven public decision datasets containing
9,212 questions that were not included in our training data.
We evaluate both Jev and our models on the same questions
for comparison. Averaged over the seven sets, the trained
31B model scores 89.0, the trained 27B model 89.4 and Jev
88.0; every open model of about 30B parameters, trained
or not, is at or above Jev. The largest set, 7,800 items from
the held-out tasks of decider, gives a clear margin over Jev:
2.2 ± 0.7 for the trained 31B model and 1.6 ± 0.7 for the
trained 27B model. The trained 31B model is also the best-

5.4. Ablations
We examine which parts of the training recipe improve per-
formance and which can be simplified. Table 5 varies one
component of the Gemma 4 31B recipe at a time, using its
three-seed mean of 84.4 Capability as the reference. Most
variants use a single run with seed 1; the training-length
experiments also include seeds 2 and 3. Reported intervals
account for question sampling and estimated training-seed
variability, so small differences should be interpreted cau-
tiously.
LoRA drives the improvement.Training only a decision
head on frozen model representations gives 78.9 Capability,
no improvement over the calibrated zero-shot score of 79.6.

its original answer-letter weights gives 84.9, comparable
to the 84.4 reference score from joint training. The main
benefit therefore comes from adapting the language model,
with no clear additional gain from training the head.

Alternative encodings and training objectives.We also
test an option encoding designed to make predictions inde-
pendent of option order, along with alternative losses for the
decision head (Appendix F). The order-invariant encoding
provides no clear benefit on Qwen3.5-4B. When training
a head on frozen representations, none of the tested losses
clearly improves calibration after temperature scaling. An
expected-utility objective, which computes the expected re-
ward for correct, incorrect, and abstained answers, improves
accuracy by 0.9 ± 0.9 percentage points over cross-entropy.
Combined with LoRA on Gemma 4 31B, the expected-
utility objective stays within the range of the recipe’s three
seeds (Appendix F).
6. Discussion and limitations
Building on existing capabilities.Our results show that
open-weight language models already achieve performance
comparable to Jevwithout any additional trainingand with-
out the need for specialized architectures or calibrated re-
inforcement learning. We show that temperature scaling
further improves the calibration of these models. These
findings reveal the latent capabilities of open-source LLMs
for probabilistic classification.
Simple LoRA fine-tuning goes beyond Jev.We show
that simple LoRA fine-tuning can improve the model’s deci-
sion capabilities even further. Our ablations suggest that the
main benefit comes from LoRA itself rather than learning
a new decision head. We show LoRA fine-tuning with the
original head fixed performs comparably to joint training.
This suggests that the simple answer-letter readout is al-
ready effective, and that improving the representations it
reads is more important in our setting. LoRA with a fixed
readout therefore provides a useful baseline for testing more
specialized decision-model designs.
Data quality and limitations.We open-source Open-
Decisions, a validation dataset for decision models. Al-
though OpenDecisions follows JevBench’s published task
composition, it may not represent all real-world applica-
tions. The dataset is 78% English, and many questions
were generated by language models rather than written by
real users. Qwen3.6-27B provides part of the training su-
pervision, while the related Qwen3.8-27B participates in
evaluation labeling, which may introduce bias. Finally, our
implementation supports at most 16 answer options.
Future work.Although our findings reveal the latent ca-
pabilities of open-source models for probabilistic classifi-
cation, this does not establish that reinforcement learning
or specialized decision architectures cannot improve per-
formance further. In fact, we believe that exploring novel
architectures, training procedures, and RL methods to fur-
ther improve performance can be an interesting avenue for
future work. Another avenue of interest is exploring which
training examples actually improve decision-making; while
training data for LLMs is abundant, more high-quality data
tailored towards decision models will likely help improve
these models further.

B. Training details
WSD branches.For the longer runs (Figures 3 and 4, Table 5) we train one run at a constant learning rate and branch a
100- to 400-step cosine decay off saved states at steps 300, 1,000, 2,000 and 3,500 (31B, seed 1). For seeds 2 and 3 of the
31B model and for the smaller models we branch 100-step decays at steps 300, 700 and 1,400, and the run itself decays
from step 2,900 to 3,000 (Qwen3.6-27B and seed 1 of Gemma 4 12B: from 1,400 to 1,500). A branch starts from the full
saved training state (weights, optimizer and data position), so it shares the main run’s first steps exactly. The 400-step recipe
is the branch at step 300; a unit test checks that a branch equals a direct run with the same schedule.
Qwen3.5-4B.The 31B recipe does not improve Qwen3.5-4B (Table 9). We tried four single changes at 400 steps (two
seeds each) and combined three of them: (i) a LoRA learning rate of 10−4 instead of 2·10 −5 (63.4 alone, against 63.0 for
the recipe); (ii) distillation: each target is the average of the pool’s target and the prediction of our trained Gemma 4 31B
(seed 1, with its fitted temperatures; 65.1 alone); (iii) a fixed half of the training questions is shown in the format of the
evaluation questions: options in a fixed order instead of shuffled, and yes/no questions with the options true and false, in this
order (63.8 alone). The fourth change, averaging each target with the zero-shot prediction, scored 64.0 and was not used.
Together, the three changes give 66.6. We chose them on OpenDecisions, so this number is optimistic in the same way as
the 31B number.

Exact overlap.We check for exact matches between training and evaluation using the complete question inputs, question
text alone, and supporting context alone. We repeat these checks after standardizing whitespace and Unicode. We find no
exact matches in any of the 2,389 evaluation questions. This rules out direct reuse of identical inputs under these checks, but
does not rule out paraphrased questions or shared underlying skills.
Similarity to training data.We also measure how closely evaluation questions resemble training questions. We hold
out 2,389 training questions (H) and use the remaining 415,436 (R) as a reference set. Using TF–IDF features and cosine
similarity, we find the closest question in R for each question in H and OpenDecisions. For this comparison, we remove
generic prompt wording and shorten the 37 longest evaluation contexts; the exact-match checks above use the full text.
The median similarity is 0.156 for OpenDecisions, compared with 0.763 for held-out training questions (Table 13). Part of
the training similarity comes from repeated supporting contexts. After excluding training questions whose context appears
exactly in R, the median falls to 0.509, still substantially above that of OpenDecisions. The difference also persists when
comparisons are restricted to the same question type.

Distinguishing the datasets.As a separate check, we train a logistic-regression classifier using TF–IDF features to
predict whether a question comes from training or evaluation. We use 2,389 questions from each dataset, grouped five-fold
cross-validation, and three random seeds. Related examples are kept in the same fold to reduce leakage, and features
are fitted only on the training folds. The classifier achieves 98.82% balanced accuracy, with a standard deviation of 0.10
percentage points across seeds, after removing the identified presentation wrappers. This shows that the datasets differ in
observable input text, although differences in wording, formatting, and task composition may contribute to the result.
Interpretation.We find no exact matches between OpenDecisions and our training pool, and the two datasets differ
substantially in their input distributions. However, this does not mean that the evaluation questions require new skills or
are necessarily harder. These differences also do not explain why models perform better or worse. We cannot rule out
paraphrased overlap or overlap with the backbones’ pretraining data. Our analysis covers the configured training pool rather
than the exact examples used in each training run. Finally, because OpenDecisions was used to select the training recipe, it
is a development set rather than an independent test set.

F. Order-invariant encoding and calibration losses
Before we settled on the recipe, we tested two other ideas on Qwen3.5-4B. Both studies use the same training pool (Table 2)
but an earlier training setup, so the numbers are not directly comparable to the main text. We rescore all runs on the seven
held-out decision sets of Table 4 (Table 16). Intervals include seed variation as in the main text: within each study we pool
the seed variance of the variants that have several seeds, and a single run counts it fully.
Order-invariant encoding.With the letter readout, the answer can depend on the order of the options. After LoRA
training, the 4B model still changes its answer on about 6% of choice and yes/no questions when the option order is reversed.
We therefore built an encoding that is order-invariant by construction. The state and question are encoded once. Each option
is then appended in its own short segment, all segments get the same positions, and no option can see the others. The score of
option j is read from the final hidden state hj of its segment as sj =w ⊤
0 hj +a ⊤std(hj) +b , where w0 is the difference of
the language-model-head rows for “yes” and “no”, anda, b start at zero, as in the decision head (Eq. (2)). The probabilities
are the softmax of the scores over the options. We tried three variants: a set layer (two attention layers without positions)
that lets the option vectors interact; a clearer wording for each option segment (Is the answer "<option>"? ); and
the same wording with all options, sorted by text, listed once in the shared part. Each run trains LoRA (rank 16 on all
projections, learning rate10 −4) and the head (learning rate10 −3) for 600 steps of 262,144 tokens, on the same batches.
All order-invariant variants score below the letter format, by 0.6 to 2.4 points. With one run per variant these differences are
within noise, so we can only say that the encoding does not help. The losses are largest on SemIf’s perturbation set and on
TypeSafe’s set, where the options must be compared with each other. Listing all options in the shared part removes most of
the gap, but then the encoding is close to a letter prompt without the letters. A simpler change gives the same invariance at
no cost: sort the options by text before building the letter prompt. The prompt is then the same for every input order of a
choice or yes/no question, and accuracy does not change (+1.0 points when we sort only at test time, −0.4 when we also
sort in training; both within noise). No variant changes the ECE by more than 0.013. We therefore keep the letter format;
sorting the options is a free option when exact invariance is needed.

G. Calibration
Table 17 compares Capability scores before and after temperature scaling, while Table 18 reports the fitted temperatures
for each question type. The largest improvements occur for the zero-shot Gemma models, whose raw probabilities are
overconfident. Temperature scaling improves Capability by 6.7 points for Gemma 4 31B and 10.0 points for Gemma 4 12B.
After fine-tuning, the fitted temperatures are much closer to one, and the improvements are smaller.
For the trained Qwen models, many fitted temperatures are below one, which makes their predictions more confident. Using
these temperatures directly reduces Capability from 81.2 to 81.0 for Qwen3.6-27B and from 66.6 to 64.6 for Qwen3.5-4B.
This illustrates that temperatures fitted on our separate calibration set do not always transfer well to OpenDecisions. We
therefore set any fitted temperature below one to one, preventing calibration from sharpening predictions. This restriction
does not affect the Gemma models and is also applied to the public systems.
Public systems.We apply the same calibration procedure to all competing systems, fitting a separate temperature for each
question type using their predictions on our held-out calibration set. As with our models, we restrict temperatures to be at
least one. For each system, we report whichever achieves higher Capability on OpenDecisions: its original predictions or its
temperature-scaled predictions (Table 19). Temperature scaling substantially improves several systems, including deck31b
(+3.2), SemIf (+3.7), Winnow-12B (+3.8), and Torchcast-decision-12B (+2.8). However, it reduces Jev’s Capability from
80.1 to 78.8, so we report Jev without additional calibration. Six other systems have all fitted temperatures below one and
are therefore unchanged by our restriction.
Where the temperatures act.Table 20 breaks the effect down for Gemma 4 31B. Temperatures never change the most
likely option, so choice and yes/no competence stay the same, and they raise calibration for every question type, most for
the zero-shot readout (choice: 58.6 to 84.0). On score questions they also lower competence, because the scorer measures it
with the expected level of the predicted distribution, which a temperature above 1 pulls toward the middle (zero-shot: 64.5
to 54.1). Across subsets, the zero-shot readout gains most on authored items (+6.6) and real requests (+6.7), and loses on the
negation pairs (100 to 94.3) and on the 35 items with exact answer probabilities. After training, the changes per subset are
small, except on these 35 items.
